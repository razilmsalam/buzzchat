# Persona: Ramesh (fictional)

> **Note**
> Simulated persona for design research. Not a real person; quotes are synthetic. Validate with real, consenting participants before relying on these findings.

A trusted elder organiser whose real network is people, paper and the morning milk centre. The expiring notice board fits how he already works. But BuzzChat as described has no voice or Marathi path, nothing for feature phones, no verifiable committee seal and no protection for young women, so it would split and endanger the community it aims to connect.

## Needs

- Voice messages in Marathi and read-aloud of notices, with a complete path that needs no typing.
- Some way for JioPhone and other button-phone households to receive notices at the same time as smartphone users, for example a "notice friend" role with loud playback.
- No data cost, low battery use and a small storage footprint.
- A seal on committee ("Samiti") notices that the other side cannot copy, so authenticity can be checked without walking an hour to the source.
- Notices that expire after the event, like paper on the samaj mandir wall.
- A plain Marathi explanation of what the app shows to others and what it hides.
- A simple Marathi "what this app is" screen he can show an officer, plus a lawyer-backed note that it is a legal messaging app.
- A separate PIN-locked space on a shared phone, so his committee messages and his granddaughter's chats stay apart and voice notes do not fill her storage.
- A way to send evidence (for example a beating video) to a trusted verifier such as a lawyer or organisation, so it reaches reporters with someone vouching for it and his name never made public.
- Protection for young women: no photo or number exposed, their name removed when they leave a group, nobody able to resend their message under their name, and an app that does not announce itself.
- Delivery that suits the village's real meeting points (milk collection centre, bus stand, ration shop, weekly bazaar), not just live crowds.
- An in-person Marathi demonstration in the samaj mandir with phones in hand, a small pilot of 5 people and 2 houses, and honest Marathi explanations when something fails.
- A way to quickly print or copy a notice for the wall.

## Deal-breakers

- Carrying the app puts young people, especially girls, at more risk than not carrying it (for example because Bluetooth shows the app is running, or family members find it).
- Anonymous posts turn into a rumour factory.
- English-only or typing-only interface.
- He cannot explain how it works to a seventy-year-old in five minutes.
- It uses data, battery or storage (especially on his granddaughter's phone).
- One of his people gets into trouble because of it.
- Failures get hidden or blamed on the user's phone instead of explained honestly.

## Design implications

1. Build signed organisational notices. A committee ("Samiti") key issues notices with a visible seal and a spoken line such as "this notice is from the Samiti, seal checked". Any change or cancellation must carry the same seal as the original, and unsealed notices claiming the same event are visibly flagged rather than shown as equals.
2. Make the app voice-first and Marathi-first: push-to-talk notice recording, Marathi text-to-speech for any text notice, a large-type mode, an interface that leans on icons, and a complete path that needs no typing.
3. Design an explicit feature-phone bridge instead of assuming the browser join works on KaiOS. Add a "notice friend" role with loud-speaker playback and a one-tap "print/copy for the wall" layout, plus an optional call/SMS relay when network exists. Test on KaiOS before claiming support, and say plainly if it is unsupported.
4. Add delay-tolerant store-and-forward for expiring notices so they move through daily meeting points (milk collection centre, bus stand, ration shop, weekly bazaar) and people carrying phones between villages. Show users the realistic delivery time (hours, not minutes).
5. Make Bluetooth presence on-demand: a clear "meeting mode" that broadcasts only while switched on, with a visible indicator, so a phone is not announcing the app around the clock. Use a neutral, non-alarming app name and icon, and explain in Marathi exactly what a nearby scanner can and cannot see.
6. Support shared phones: a separate PIN-locked space per person, app-only storage with a size cap, voice notes never saved to the gallery, and short retention by default.
7. Make low retention the safety model instead of a panic wipe: notices expire and old messages auto-delete. If a wipe exists, it lives in settings, clears only BuzzChat data, says so in Marathi before confirming, and cannot touch other apps' files.
8. Ship a lawyer-reviewed, plain-Marathi "What is this app" screen a user can show an officer, plus a printable explainer letter for organisers.
9. Protect identity by default for vulnerable users: no profile photos, no numbers, temporary nicknames, the name removed from a group when the person leaves, and limits or warnings on re-sharing someone's message under their name. Be honest that screenshots cannot be prevented.
10. Rework "share to internet" as "send to a verifier". Evidence goes to a pre-chosen trusted lawyer or organisation, who can attach "checked: region, this week" before it reaches reporters. Attach capture-date information to counter recycled old videos, and visibly mark unvouched anonymous posts as unverified.
11. Limit spread of unverified posts: no read-aloud or alert-style delivery for unsealed posts, a "seen with my own eyes?" prompt before forwarding, and download-on-request for media to keep data and storage costs down.
12. Supply an onboarding and pilot kit: a facilitator script for in-person Marathi demos in community halls, a five-minute explainer suitable for a 70-year-old, a small test mode (for example 5 people, 2 houses) with delivery receipts, and honest Marathi failure messages that name the cause (distance, Bluetooth off, phone, app).
13. Keep consent at the centre: no pressure to install, and members who decline (especially young women) can still get notices through a notice friend without carrying the app.
14. Set battery and data budgets for cheap Android phones in areas with power cuts: a low-power scanning schedule, battery-use transparency, and no internet bridging without explicit opt-in and a data cap.

## Interview transcript

### Q1. Who you are

My name is Ramesh. Sixty-three years old. I worked thirty-one years as a clerk in the taluka office, typing land records and stamping files. I retired five years ago. Now I live in my village in Marathwada with my son, my daughter-in-law and two grandchildren. My wife passed four years back.

Morning I water the tulsi, read the Marathi newspaper slowly with my glasses, and then people start coming. Someone's ration card is cancelled. Someone's land is being measured wrongly. A boy was beaten at the well and the police will not write the FIR. Because I was in government service, they think I know the *kaydā* (law). Some of it I do know. I run legal-awareness meetings in the *samaj mandir* (community hall), and two or three times a year we hold peaceful rallies on 14 April and after any atrocity case. We read the Constitution aloud. Babasaheb gave us that book, so we use it.

I do this because when I was a boy I could not drink from the same pot as others in school. My grandchildren should not have to explain that to their own children.

### Q2. Your phone(s)

I have a JioPhone, the small one with buttons. It cost around one thousand five hundred rupees. It has some apps. WhatsApp is there but slow, and the screen is very small for my eyes. Mostly I use it for calls. The recharge is about one hundred and fifty to two hundred rupees a month. My pension is not big and that money matters.

The battery lasts two days if I don't touch WhatsApp. Light here goes for four or five hours some days, so I charge whenever there is light. Typing Marathi on the buttons is very painful. I press 2 three times to get one letter. So I send voice notes, or I just call.

Sometimes I use my granddaughter Sneha's Android. She is in eleventh standard. It's a Redmi, maybe nine or ten thousand rupees, and the storage is always full of her photos and her exam PDFs. She complains when I install anything. She also does not like me reading her messages, which is fair. On that phone I use YouTube for news, and WhatsApp groups. There is our *Sanvidhan Jagruti* (Constitution awareness) group with about two hundred members.

### Q3. Your worst shutdown or network failure

There was no official government shutdown in our village. It was worse in a way, because nobody announced it. After a clash in a nearby village over a wedding procession, the mobile signal was nearly dead for about three days. Maybe a tower problem, maybe something else. Nobody told us.

First day, evening: we heard from a man on a motorcycle that our people's houses there had been attacked. I tried calling. Calls dropped. WhatsApp showed one tick for hours.

That night we wanted to hold a peaceful sit-in at the tehsil office the next morning and give a memorandum to the officer. I had to tell people. So I sent boys on bicycles to five *wadis* (hamlets) with handwritten chits. It took the whole night.

Second day: forty people came instead of four hundred. Many never got the message. Others got a wrong one, because someone said the rally was cancelled. Who said that? Nobody knows.

Third day: signal came back partly. Then fifty forwarded videos arrived at once, half of them from other states and years old. My son spent two hundred rupees extra on data that week, only on rubbish videos.

What it cost: the memorandum was delayed. A family who needed a lawyer that first night did not get one until the fourth day. That is the real cost.

### Q4. Safety and fear

I am not a young man with a mobile full of secrets. But fear is there. After the rally, a man from the dominant community stopped me outside the bank and said, very politely, "*Kaka* (uncle), at your age you should rest." I understood what he meant.

There are informers inside our own groups. In a two-hundred-member WhatsApp group, someone always forwards screenshots to the sarpanch's people. So in the group I write only what I would say in front of a police officer: dates, the time of the meeting, the legal section. The private talk I do face to face.

The police took one young man's phone after a protest two years ago. They kept it for weeks. Since then I keep very little on my phone. I don't have a delete habit; I just don't put things there in the first place.

What worries me more is the young women in our movement. When they post, they get filthy comments with caste abuse. One girl's photo was edited and sent around. Her family made her stop. That is how they silence us now. They don't need a lathi.

Who do I trust? People I have known for twenty years. My son. Sneha. Not a phone.

### Q5. Information problems

Rumours are the biggest weapon. In our area a fake message can bring a mob in one hour. Once a message went around that "Dalit boys insulted a statue." It was false. I had to go to the police station myself at midnight with two elders to stop it from spreading.

How do I verify? I call someone I know who lives in that village. If the call does not go through, I wait. I tell people *"dolyāne pāhilyāshivāy forward karu naka"* (do not forward unless you saw it with your own eyes). Nobody listens.

Getting news out is hard. The district reporters mostly know the other side's leaders. If a beating happens and there is no video, it did not happen as far as the media is concerned. Getting news in, I rely on the newspaper, which is a day late, and phone calls.

### Q6. Reaction to the BuzzChat concept

(The interviewer explained it in Marathi: messages go phone to phone without internet, you can join through a QR code or a link, public notices, private locked messages, a wipe button.)

What I like: the notice board idea. A notice for a meeting that disappears after the meeting is over is good. That is what we do with the paper on the samaj mandir wall. If the signal goes and the notice still reaches the next phone, and the next, that is like my boys on bicycles but faster.

What confuses me: my JioPhone. You say "open in a browser". I think my phone has a browser, but I have never scanned a QR code with it. Will it work on my phone or not? And you say only near a "hub phone". So then I must stand near Sneha's phone. Then it's her phone, not mine.

Then there is Bluetooth, tens of metres. Our wadis are one or two kilometres apart. In a rally crowd, fine. In the village at night, there are fifteen houses with smartphones, spread out. Will it reach? I doubt it.

What scares me: you said anyone scanning can see the app is running. So the police or the other side's boys will know I have the "protest app". In our village everybody already knows who I am, but a young girl with this app on her phone is now marked. That worries me.

And the wipe button: if a policeman sees me pressing it, that itself looks like guilt. And if Sneha presses it by mistake, her exam notes are gone? I need to understand that.

"Share to internet hidden through Tor": I don't understand Tor. But if a beating happens and the video reaches a reporter in Mumbai without my name, that I want. Only, who checks that it is true? This app could also carry rumours faster. A false message with no name on it is more dangerous than one with a name.

What would stop me: if it needs typing in English. If it eats data or battery. If it fills Sneha's storage. If one of my people gets into trouble because of it.

Before I trust it, I want a person I know to show me, in Marathi, in the samaj mandir, with the phones in our hands. Not a YouTube video. And I want a lawyer or someone from our side to say whether keeping it on the phone can be used against us.

What would I tell others? "Try it in the meeting first. Not in a crisis."

### Q7. Must-haves and deal-breakers

**Must-haves, in order:**
1. Voice messages in Marathi, and reading aloud for people like me with weak eyes. No typing needed.
2. It must work on cheap phones, including some way for the JioPhone people, or it divides our community into smartphone and button-phone people.
3. No data cost, very little battery, small size.
4. A way to know a notice really came from our committee. Some mark or seal that the sarpanch's boys cannot copy.
5. Notices that expire, like the wall paper.
6. Clear Marathi explanation of what the app shows to others and what it hides.

**Deal-breakers:**
1. If carrying it puts the young people, especially the girls, at more risk than not carrying it.
2. If anonymous posts become a factory for rumours.
3. English-only, or typing-only.
4. If I cannot explain how it works to a seventy-year-old in five minutes.

We are not against technology. We are against tools that end up working for the powerful. Show me it works for the weak first.

## Follow-up interview

### Follow-up 1. You said you sent boys on bicycles with handwritten chits, and then a message that the sit-in was cancelled spread among people. Can you take me through how people decided which message to believe that night? What made a chit or a word of mouth feel real or not real to them?

People did not believe the paper. They believed the boy who carried it. In Bhimnagar wadi they know my grandson's friend Sachin, so when he came, they believed him. In the far wadi I sent a boy nobody knew well, and they asked, "Who sent you? Show us." The chit had my handwriting and my signature, the same one I used on government files for thirty-one years. Some old people know that signature, but the young ones do not.

The "cancelled" message came by mouth. Someone said, "Ramesh Kaka himself said it is off because of police." People believed it because it sounded careful, and careful sounds like me. Also, a cancellation is easier to believe. It asks nothing of you. You just stay home.

The people who came were the ones who checked. One woman sent her son to my house at six in the morning to ask me directly. That is the only real test we have: go to the source. It took her an hour of walking.

### Follow-up 2. You trust people you have known for twenty years, and you said a nameless message is more dangerous than one with a name. You also want a beating video to reach a Mumbai reporter without your name on it. How do you think about when a name should travel with a message and when it should not?

I have thought about this since you asked. I think it depends on who is asking people to act.

If a message says "come to the tehsil at ten," people will leave their fields and their wages. Someone must answer for that, so the committee's name must be on it. Not my personal name, maybe, but a name people can hold responsible. That is how a notice on the samaj mandir wall works: it says *Samiti*, and everyone knows who the Samiti is.

A video of a beating is different. The video is the evidence, not me. It does not need my name. But the reporter still needs to know it is not fake. So somebody has to vouch for it. Maybe a lawyer from our side, or a known organisation, someone who checks it and says "this is from Marathwada, this week." The name doesn't have to travel to the public. It has to travel to the one person who checks.

So I mean hidden from the enemy, not hidden from everybody. Truly nameless, from nobody, is how rumours travel.

### Follow-up 3. Think of the young women in your movement, like the girl whose photo was edited. If one of them were sitting here, what do you think she would want to know before keeping an app like this on her phone? Who else in her life would have a say?

I should not speak for her completely. You should ask Priyanka and the other girls themselves, not an old man. But from what I have seen, I think she would first ask: "Can anyone see my photo or my number through this?" Her photo is what they used against that girl. After that: "If I leave a group, does my name stay there?" And: "Can someone take my message and spread it with my name?"

She would also want to know whether the app shows up when her brother or father looks at the phone. In many homes the girl's phone is not fully hers. The father checks it. Sometimes the brother does too. If he sees a "protest app," there will be a fight, and maybe the phone is taken away. Her mother has a say quietly. If the family is worried about her marriage prospects, the mother will stop her before anyone else does.

And the in-laws, if she is married. That is a different house with different rules.

So it is not only a technical question for her. She would want something that does not shout what it is.

### Follow-up 4. Right now Sneha's phone holds her photos, her exam notes and sometimes your WhatsApp. If an app for your movement were on her phone, how would you two decide what is hers and what is yours? What would happen at home if something went wrong?

Honestly, we would sit at dinner and she would set the rules, not me. She already says, "Ajoba, don't touch the gallery." Maybe the app could have a separate locked part for me, with my own number lock, so she does not see my committee messages and I do not see her friends' chats. She would like that. She would also want it to be small and not to save every voice note into her storage.

If something went wrong, say the police came asking or a message from my group was found on her phone, then my son would be angry with me, not with her. He would say, "Baba, you put your work on a child's phone." He would be right. She is in eleventh standard, and her board exams are next year. If her phone were taken for weeks, her studies would suffer, and that is my fault.

So honestly, maybe the movement app should not be on Sneha's phone at all. Maybe I need my own cheap Android, even a second-hand one for three thousand rupees. Her phone is for her future.

### Follow-up 5. About people seeing that the app is running and someone watching you press the wipe button: in that situation, what would feel safer to you? Having the app there and being ready to explain it, or not having it at all? What makes you lean one way or the other?

For me, having it and being ready to explain it. I am sixty-three and a retired government servant. Everyone already knows my work. I have nothing to hide that is against the law. If someone asks, I can say, "This is for meeting notices when the network is gone. Here, read it." What I would want is a simple screen that explains in Marathi what the app is, something I could show an officer. Also a letter from our lawyer saying it is a legal messaging app, which I would keep in my pocket.

I would not press a wipe button in front of anyone. It looks like you are destroying evidence, and that creates a bigger problem than whatever was there. My habit is to keep nothing sensitive on the phone in the first place.

For a young boy or girl it is different. They have more to lose and less standing. For them I lean toward not having it unless the whole group understands it first. The difference is how much protection a person already has. I have some. They have little.

### Follow-up 6. For the JioPhone families in your village, what would be the least they need so they do not feel left out? Hearing a notice played aloud? Getting a call or SMS from someone who has it? Something else?

The least is a person. One person in each wadi with a smartphone, a "notice friend," who receives the notice and makes sure the button-phone houses hear it. Most of them will not read an SMS anyway. Many are older, or the women have not studied much. A voice call works best. Even a missed call system, where they give a missed call and hear the notice, the way the old government schemes did. But that needs network, I know.

When there is no network, the person walks over and plays the voice note aloud. If the app could make the notice easy to play loud, in Marathi, with a clear voice saying "this is from the Samiti," that already helps. Also a way to print or write it out quickly for the wall.

What they must not feel is that the smartphone people decided everything and told them later. So the notice should reach them at the same time, through the notice friend, not the next day. Being informed late feels the same as being left out.

### Follow-up 7. When you picture fifteen smartphone houses spread across wadis a kilometre or two apart, where in the village do people already meet or pass by during the day and at night?

In the morning, the milk collection centre. Everybody's buffalo milk goes there between six and eight, from all the wadis. Then the bus stand on the main road. The boys going to college in the taluka town wait there, and the bus driver and conductor carry news between villages every day. Also the ration shop when grain comes, and the flour mill.

During the day the women are at the hand pump and the water tanker. The men are in the fields, and that is scattered.

In the evening, the samaj mandir, the small tea stall near the Buddha *vihar*, and the temple side for the other communities, which our people do not go to much. On Sundays there is the weekly bazaar in the bigger village, where all the surrounding villages come.

At night everyone is at home and the wadis are cut off from each other. Nobody walks between them after dark, especially our people, especially during tension. So at night, honestly, nothing connects us. The milk centre in the morning is our real network.

### Follow-up 8. You said: try it in the meeting first, not in a crisis. If a trial happened in the samaj mandir and it went badly, for example messages did not arrive or someone was worried afterwards, what would you need to see before trying again?

First, an honest explanation. Not "it's your phone's fault." Tell me plainly in Marathi what failed and why, whether it was the distance, the phone or the app. If they hide it or make excuses, I will not trust them again. A clerk knows when a file is being covered up.

Second, if someone was worried, a young woman for example, I would need someone to sit with her and answer her questions, and maybe our lawyer too. Her worry counts more than the technology. If she says no, we respect that, and nobody pressures her.

Third, I would want to see it working in a small test before calling people again. Say five people, two houses, my son and me. If messages arrive there, then try the full meeting.

And people's time. A meeting costs them a half day's wages. I would not call them a second time unless I was quite sure. If it fails twice, people will not come a third time, and they will not believe me next time I say "new tool." I can afford to lose a phone, but I cannot afford to lose people's trust in my word.
