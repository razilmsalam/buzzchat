## Needs

- Short text notices that reliably arrive nearby and cost little battery, e.g. "meet at library", "first aid at steps", "march turning back".
- Notices with roles on them, like the printed sheet (first aid, legal contact, media, marshals), set up in person on the morning of the march.
- A correction attached directly on top of the original notice. It should state a specific fact and come from a role-holder or someone who is physically nearby, not from anonymous votes.
- Fast fading of stale notices, so 40-minute-old panic does not stay at the top.
- Layered spaces: a public "loudspeaker" layer and a small private layer joined by scanning phones in person. The current layer should be shown in big, obvious words every time.
- A rough count of how many people can read a post, without showing who they are.
- An extra confirmation step before anything is shared to the internet.
- Automatic face blurring and location-data stripping before anything goes outward.
- A way to vouch for footage without revealing her identity or the original location, e.g. "verified by a known group" (student journalism collective, legal aid), and a way to link matching clips from other angles.
- Original-quality short clips and photos (not compressed) passed to whoever finds signal first.
- A shared "safe / not yet seen" check-in that only trusted marshals can update.
- A clear statement of whether it works on her old iPhone, plus a one-sentence explanation of iPhone app vs browser vs hub, and whether bitchat and BuzzChat interoperate.
- A low-power mode, and control over how much storage other people's media can use.
- A Hindi interface by default (other Indian languages too), voice notes, a big microphone button, and a spoken explainer for less-literate or non-English users.
- An ordinary, honest app identity and a "why people use this" screen in Hindi she can show her mother or an officer.
- Honest limits on the first screen: no livestreaming, Bluetooth is detectable, and the wipe does not clear the camera roll.
- An independent security review by people she respects and code a trusted CS friend can read; a campus test with 20 people before any real day.

## Deal-breakers

- It makes her more identifiable than not using it (Bluetooth visibility or anything else) without telling her plainly.
- The wipe says "wiped" while photos are still on the phone (false confidence).
- It becomes a rumour machine with no way to correct or flag false notices.
- It needs a phone number, an account, or an app-store install on the day.
- Battery drops from about 100% to 60% within the first hour.
- It quietly fills her already-full storage with other people's media.
- It asks for permissions without explaining why.
- It looks like a spy gadget (disguised icon, hacker language) or claims she is "invisible" when she is not.
- The first group test ends in "is it working? I can't see anything" for half an hour.

## Design implications

1. Put an honest limits screen first, in plain Hindi and English: no livestreaming, nearby phones can tell the app is running, range needs a crowd, and the wipe does not clear the camera roll.
2. Write a one-sentence iPhone explainer (app vs browser vs hub, bitchat compatibility) and show hub status in the browser client: who the hub is, signal strength, and a clear "you are disconnected" state instead of a silent empty feed.
3. Show which layer a post goes to with a large, colour-coded label (PUBLIC – anyone nearby / PRIVATE – your group) on every compose screen, plus a rough count of who can read it.
4. Make "share to internet" a deliberate two-step action with a preview. Strip location and other metadata by default and blur faces on the device before release, and say clearly that blurring can miss faces.
5. Explore credibility without identity: an optional group signature from a pre-agreed collective (journalism or legal aid), a timestamp and content hash, and linking of matching clips from different angles. Keep original location data out of it, and warn that vouching groups can become targets.
6. Let corrections attach to the original notice. Offer "I'm here: confirm" and "I'm here: not happening" markers based on direct nearby reception rather than forwarding, if that can be done without leaking location. Prompt corrections to state a specific fact, and fade notices aggressively.
7. Add organiser-assigned role tags (marshal, first aid, legal, media), set face-to-face on the morning of the march like the printed sheet, and let role-holders' notices and corrections stand out.
8. Add a "safe / not yet seen" roster in the private layer that only designated marshals can update, so the contact person can answer parents.
9. Design for an old iPhone: a strict low-power mode that is text-only by default, user-controlled media auto-download, and a storage cap on relayed media.
10. Build voice-first: a big push-to-talk microphone button, Hindi as the default with other Indian languages, a spoken intro on first open, and no jargon ("mesh", "relay") in the main UI.
11. Give the app an honest identity: a plain name and icon, no disguise mode, and a shareable Hindi "why people use this" screen for family.
12. Have the wipe report exactly what it removed and what it cannot touch (camera roll, other apps). Never imply complete erasure.
13. Provide a campus-test kit (a 20-person drill script and an obvious "I can see N people nearby" indicator) so the first group experience succeeds.
14. Publish a security review and readable source so trusted local technical friends can vouch for it; that personal trust chain drives adoption more than marketing does.

## Interview transcript

### Q1. Who you are

I'm Priya. I'm 22 and in my final year of a social sciences degree at a university in Delhi. My parents live in a smaller town in UP, and I share a flat in the city with two other girls. Most days I have classes in the morning, tutor two school kids in the evening for some extra money, and fit in reading-group meetings and organising work wherever I can.

I started in my second year. A hostel curfew rule only applied to women, so we held a sit-in about it. After that I got pulled into solidarity marches on fee hikes, on students who were detained, and on campus speech rules. Everything we do is peaceful. We make posters, read the Preamble aloud, and sing. My mother calls it my "andolan phase" (protest phase). She's half joking and half scared. My father doesn't really want to know. Honestly, I'm not sure I'm brave. I mostly hate being told to stay quiet.

### Q2. Your phone(s)

I have an old iPhone that my cousin handed down to me when she upgraded. Battery health is around 74%, and on a march day it's dead by 3 pm. The storage is 64 GB and always full. Half of it is protest photos I'm too scared to upload and too sentimental to delete. I carry a cheap power bank that takes forever to charge. I also have a second-hand laptop, but it's heavy and I don't take it to protests.

My prepaid plan costs about ₹300-something a month for 1.5 GB a day. That's fine until I try to livestream, and then it's gone in an hour. Nobody else uses the phone, but my roommates know my passcode in case something happens to me.

The apps I use are Signal for organising, Instagram for outreach, WhatsApp for family (I can't escape it), Google Maps, and UPI. I type in English or Hinglish. For my nani (grandmother) I send Hindi voice notes. A lot of the people in our wider networks, like canteen workers and the families of detained students, prefer voice to text.

### Q3. Your worst shutdown or network failure

It was last year, on a march day near campus. I won't give the exact date.

- **Around 11 am:** We gathered at the gate and everything was normal. I posted a story.
- **Around 12:30:** Mobile data dropped for everyone in the area at the same moment, so it wasn't my phone. Calls worked badly and SMS sort of worked. Signal just showed the clock icon.
- **1 to 3 pm:** We fell back on shouting, runners, and a printed list of phone numbers that one senior had insisted we carry. I thought he was being dramatic, and he wasn't. We had made a plan to meet near the library if separated, but half the people never got that message because it was only posted on Signal the night before.
- **3 pm:** Some students were detained. We didn't know who or where. Parents were calling the one number that worked, and it was my number. I couldn't confirm anything to anyone.
- **Evening:** Data came back patchily after we'd walked far enough away. By then rumours were everywhere: someone had been badly hurt, or the march had been "violent." Neither was true.

It cost us the story. Our footage reached journalists the next morning, and by then the other version had already spread. It also cost my phone battery, my voice, and probably a year off my mother's life.

### Q4. Safety and fear

What scares me most isn't the police. It's being identified and then trolled. Women activists get their photos morphed, their home addresses guessed, and their families' names posted. A friend's face got circled in a viral photo with a caption calling her "anti-national." The comments were about her body, not her politics. She deleted all her accounts for six months.

So I check who's in a photo's background before I post it. I strip location data. I keep my Instagram handle unrelated to my real name, though a determined person could still link it. Signal has disappearing messages on for our core group. Sometimes I worry the phone gets checked at a barricade, and I don't really know what I'd do if that happened. I don't want to be the person who looks like they're hiding something.

The people I trust are about eight people in real life. Large groups always have someone screenshotting. I'm not saying that's an informer. Sometimes it's just someone who's scared and wants to show their parents they "weren't involved."

### Q5. Information problems

The rumours spread in both directions. Our side spreads exaggerations too. Someone once forwarded "lathi charge at Gate 3!!" and it turned out to be a scuffle over a banner, so we lost credibility. I verify by calling someone I know is physically there, if calls work at all.

Getting news out is the main thing for me. Journalists want raw, timestamped footage from a source they can trust, not a random forward. During that shutdown, we had video and no way to move it. Getting news in matters too: is the metro station closed, are people being detained at a particular spot, where is the lawyer?

### Q6. Reaction to the BuzzChat concept

*(Interviewer explained: messages hop phone-to-phone over Bluetooth/Wi-Fi without internet; join by QR code or link in a browser with no account; posts marked "share to internet" get pushed out via Tor by anyone nearby who has connectivity; an expiring area notice board; encrypted private messages; animated QR codes for screen-to-camera transfer; an emergency wipe; with limits on range, jamming, browser-only hub dependence, and visibility to Bluetooth scanners.)*

**What excites me:** the "share to internet" part. If even one person at the edge of the crowd gets signal, our version gets out. And no phone number? That's huge. Our phone numbers are basically our identities here. The notice board that expires is exactly the "meet at library" problem from my shutdown story. I also like the QR-from-a-friend's-phone joining, because getting 200 people to install an app on the morning of a march will never happen.

**What confuses me:** I have an iPhone. So do I use the browser or the bitchat app? If it's the browser, you're saying it only works near a "hub phone." Who is the hub? What if the hub person leaves or their battery dies? And are the bitchat app and BuzzChat even the same network? I'd need that explained in one sentence, not a paragraph.

**What scares me:** "Anyone scanning Bluetooth can tell the app is running." So if police scan a crowd, the people running it stand out? That could make it a marker. I'm also not convinced about the emergency wipe. If my phone gets checked and the app is already gone, does that look worse? And on my old iPhone, does the wipe even clear photos I've already saved to the camera roll? Probably not, right? People will assume it does.

**Also:** I can't livestream over this, can I? Video through Bluetooth hops sounds impossible. I'd like someone to tell me that honestly instead of letting me find out on the day. And if a post gets "shared to the internet" anonymously, why would a journalist believe it? Anonymity protects me but it hurts credibility. I don't know how you solve both.

**What would stop me:** if it drains my already-dying battery, if it looks sketchy and my less-techy friends don't trust it, or if it becomes a channel for fake panic messages that nobody can trace or correct.

**What I'd need before trusting it:** a security review by people I actually respect, and a friend from the CS department who has read the code and tells me it's okay. I'd also need a test with 20 people on campus before any real day. The app should be in Hindi too, with voice notes.

**What I'd tell others:** "Keep it as a backup, not your main plan. Still carry the printed list."

### Q7. Must-haves and deal-breakers

**Must-haves, in order:**
1. Works properly on an old iPhone, or tells me clearly that it won't.
2. Automatic face blurring and location-data stripping before anything gets shared outward.
3. A way for journalists to trust footage without me revealing who I am. Even a "verified by a known group" option would help.
4. Battery use I can actually live with, plus a low-power mode.
5. Expiring notices for logistics, and a way to correct or flag false ones.
6. Hindi interface and voice notes.

**Deal-breakers:**
1. It makes me more identifiable than not using it, through Bluetooth visibility or anything else, without telling me plainly.
2. The wipe gives false confidence, meaning it says "wiped" while photos are still on the phone.
3. It turns into a rumour machine with no way to correct anything.
4. It needs a phone number, an account, or an app-store install on the day.

Honestly? If you fix the trust problem with journalists, I'd use it. If you don't, it's just another group chat that dies when I need it.

## Follow-up interview

### Follow-up 1. You said the printed list of phone numbers was the thing that actually worked that day, even though you'd thought it was dramatic. Can you take me back to the moment you first used it? What made it feel usable and trustworthy when everything else had failed?

It was probably around 1:15. I was standing near a tea stall and pressing refresh on Signal over and over, like that would do anything. The senior who'd handed out the list, I'll call him Bhaiya, just said "list nikalo" (take out the list). It was folded in my jeans pocket and a bit sweaty. It was one A4 sheet with about thirty names, and each name had a role next to it: "first aid," "legal contact," "media," "gate 2 coordinator."

What made it work was that it was so boring. You didn't need a battery or an app for it, and you didn't have to wonder whether it was outdated, because he'd printed it that morning. The roles mattered more than the names. I didn't need to know who Rohit was. I needed whoever was doing "legal," and the sheet told me.

It also felt trustworthy because I knew who made it. One person I'd seen with my own eyes put it in my hand. Nobody forwarded it to me. When I think about BuzzChat now, I think that's the feeling it has to copy: something small and current, with roles on it, and from someone I know.

### Follow-up 2. You mentioned you trust about eight people in real life, and you also liked the idea of 200 people joining from a QR code on a friend's phone. How do you think about who ends up in the same space as you on a day like that? What would you want to know, or control, about who can see what you post?

I hold both of those at once and they contradict each other, I know. On a march day the crowd is everyone: students, curious people, people from other colleges, sometimes plainclothes people, sometimes people who just want content. I can't control who's physically there, so I don't expect to control who's in a digital space that's tied to the area.

I don't want one group. I want layers. There'd be a public layer, like a loudspeaker, where I assume anyone could read it, including people who don't like us. That's for "water at the library steps" and "march moving left." Then a small layer for my eight, which only we can open, maybe joined by scanning each other's phones in person.

What I'd want to know is which layer I'm posting into, in big obvious words, every single time, because I'll be tired and panicking. I'd also want to see roughly how many people can read something, not who they are. If a post is going to be "shared to internet," I want an extra step before it goes. Mistakes on a day like that aren't small mistakes.

### Follow-up 3. Think about the footage that reached journalists the next morning. What happened between you having the video and a journalist trusting it? Who vouched for it, and what did they ask you for? And if a 'verified by a known group' badge existed, who would you be comfortable having that power, and what would worry you about it?

It happened in a messy chain. I got home, charged the phone and sent the clips to a friend who's in a student journalism collective. She knew a reporter from an earlier story. So the reporter trusted her, she trusted me, and that was the whole verification.

The reporter asked for the original files and not the WhatsApp-compressed versions, because she wanted the metadata. She asked what time it was shot and where I was standing. She asked whether anything before or after had been cut. She asked whether there was anyone else who'd filmed it from another angle, which is the part I've thought about since. Two clips from different phones matching each other was worth more than my word.

For the badge, I'd be comfortable with a student journalism collective or a legal-aid group, the kind of people whose whole reputation depends on not lying. I'd be worried if it became a political party's stamp, or one person who suddenly has power over what's "true." I'd also worry that a badge becomes a target. If people know which group verifies things, that group's members get trolled or pressured. And I'd want a way to vouch for footage without the original location data going along with it.

### Follow-up 4. You said you don't want to be the person who looks like they're hiding something, and you also worry the app could show up in a Bluetooth scan or that a missing app could look worse. If you picture your phone being looked at, at a barricade or by family, what would feel safest to you? What would feel worse than not having the app at all?

Honestly, what would feel safest is an app that looks like exactly what it is: a messaging tool for when the network is down. People use it during floods and power cuts too. If it looks normal, I can explain it in one sentence, to an officer or to my mother, without sounding guilty. I'm not doing anything illegal. I just want to be able to say "this is how we found each other when data stopped."

What would feel worse than not having it is anything that looks like a spy gadget. Strange icons, fake calculator disguises, or scary hacker language would turn a normal student into a suspicious one. Also anything that tells me "you're invisible" when I'm not. That kind of false confidence is worse than no app.

With family, the problem isn't hiding. My mother scrolls through my phone when I visit home, and seeing something she doesn't understand makes her panic. So a clear name, a clear explanation inside the app in Hindi, and maybe a simple "why people use this" screen I could literally hand her. Saying it plainly protects me more than hiding does.

### Follow-up 5. You were candid that your own side sometimes spreads exaggerations, like the 'lathi charge at Gate 3' forward. On a notice board with no names attached, how would you want a wrong or panicky notice to get noticed and corrected? Whose correction would you believe, and whose wouldn't you?

I don't think anonymous voting works. Ten panicked people upvoting "lathi charge!!" doesn't make it true, it just makes it louder. What I'd want is for people to be able to mark "I'm physically here and I see this," or "I'm here and this isn't happening." Even without names, if the app could show that the person is nearby and not forwarding from far away, that would mean something.

A correction should sit right on top of the original, not as a separate post that half the people never see. Old notices should fade quickly too. Panic from forty minutes ago shouldn't still be at the top.

I'd believe corrections from the role people: the first-aid team, the legal contact, the marshals we picked that morning. If those roles could be marked somehow, the way the printed list did it, that's who I'd trust. I wouldn't believe a correction that comes from nowhere and says "everything is calm, go home." That's exactly what someone who wants to break up a protest would post. A correction should give a specific fact, like "Gate 3: banner argument, no police, resolved." A plain "don't worry" isn't a correction.

### Follow-up 6. At 3 pm, yours was the one number that worked, and parents were calling you while you couldn't confirm anything. Looking back, what would you most have wanted to be able to tell them, or to find out, in that hour? How did carrying that affect you?

I just wanted to be able to say "your son is with the group" or "we don't know yet, but the legal contact is going to the station." Even "we don't know" with a plan attached would have been better than me saying "Aunty, I'm trying" twelve times. One mother called me five times. Her voice got quieter each time, and that was worse than if she'd shouted.

What I needed to find out was a list: who was confirmed safe and who hadn't been seen. Something shared that the marshals could update as they found people, so I wasn't trying to remember names in my head while my phone was at 9%.

It affected me more than I admitted. That night I couldn't sleep, and for weeks afterwards a call from an unknown number made my stomach drop. I stopped volunteering to be a contact person for a while. I think people building tools forget the person in the middle who has to say "I don't know" to a crying parent. If BuzzChat could help with a "safe / not yet seen" check-in that only trusted people can update, that alone would matter.

### Follow-up 7. Your phone is dead by 3 pm, the storage is full, and data runs out in an hour of livestreaming. If BuzzChat could realistically do only one or two things well on that phone, which would matter most? What would make you uninstall it after the first try?

If it can only do two things, I'd pick these. First, short text notices nearby that actually arrive: "meet at library," "first aid at steps," "march turning back." Small, fast, and cheap on battery. Second, getting a short clip or a few photos out through whoever finds signal first, with faces blurred and location stripped. A thirty-second clip that reaches a journalist that afternoon is worth more than a livestream nobody can watch.

I've accepted livestreaming isn't going to happen over Bluetooth. Just tell me that on the first screen and I'll respect you for it.

What would make me uninstall it after the first try: if I open it at 10 am and my battery is at 60% by 11. If it quietly fills my storage with other people's media. If it asks me for some permission and I don't understand why. And if the campus test with 20 people turns into "is it working? I can't see anything" for half an hour. One bad first experience and my friends won't open it again, and then I'm on a network by myself.

### Follow-up 8. You mentioned that friends from the canteen workers to detained students' families prefer voice to text, and that you send Hindi voice notes to your nani. If BuzzChat were offered to those people rather than to students like you, what do you imagine happening when they try it?

Honestly? I imagine Raju bhaiya from the canteen (not his real name) holding the phone at arm's length and saying "isme toh kuch aa hi nahi raha" (nothing's showing up in this). Most of them use cheaper Android phones, which might actually work better than my iPhone, but the problem won't be the technology. It'll be English words like "mesh," "relay," "verify."

If the first thing they see is a QR code and a browser page, many will think it's a scam link, because they've been warned about exactly that kind of thing on WhatsApp. Families of detained students are scared and suspicious, and they're right to be.

What would work: a big microphone button, and Hindi (and other languages, not just Hindi) as the default. A short voice explanation that plays when you open it. Voice notes that work without reading anything. And it should come from someone they already trust, like me sitting next to them, the way I set up UPI for my nani.

I think they'd use it more than students would, if it felt like a walkie-talkie and not an app. Voice is how most of them already communicate.
