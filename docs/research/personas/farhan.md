# Persona: Farhan (fictional)

> **Note**
> Simulated persona for design research. Not a real person; quotes are synthetic. Validate with real, consenting participants before relying on these findings.

**In one line:** A freelance reporter whose job is getting verifiable evidence out, not chatting. BuzzChat's anonymous, mostly text, crowd-dependent design helps the people around him but fails him unless it adds signed provenance, media support, source protection that travels with the file, and a low profile.

Farhan (fictional), 35, is a freelance journalist and citizen reporter who moves between Guwahati and Lucknow covering protests, evictions and student agitations in Assam and Uttar Pradesh. He has freelanced for nine years and earns an irregular income from digital outlets and the occasional wire desk. His wife's teaching salary is the household's stable income, and they have a six-year-old daughter. He insists he is a reporter, not a protester, but both sides often treat him as being on the other one. He faces online harassment tied to his Muslim name and fully trusts about five people. His formative experience is the December 2019 Assam shutdown. He lost two exclusives and about ₹15,000, and a source was exposed when an outlet published an uncropped raw clip he had sent. He constantly has to choose between keeping metadata to prove footage is real and stripping it to protect the people in it.

## Needs

- Optional signing of chosen posts and files with a stable press identity, kept separate from anonymous posting, plus key verification done in person so an editor sees "signed by known contact" rather than a typed name.
- Tamper-evident provenance: proof that a file is unchanged since signing even after many hops, a clear mark of original versus compressed copy, and "original held by sender".
- Media support: compressed video clips sent quickly, with the full-quality original queued for later or requested on demand.
- Source protection by default: one-tap stripping of faces and metadata on export, and protection labels (for example "source: protect") that travel inside the file and warn the recipient when it is opened.
- Per-clip choice of what to keep (time only, time and location, nothing), quick enough to make in about ten seconds in a crowd.
- Selective protection in tiers: source identities first, app contacts and chats second, footage last and never deleted or altered, with an honest statement of what the app can and cannot erase.
- App data that never reaches or modifies the camera roll, and a clear, plain explanation of exactly what is stored on the phone and where.
- A bulletin board with meaningful trust signals (confirmations from nearby people, verified medics or volunteers), visible post age, fading of old posts, and an "unconfirmed" label by default.
- "News in": bulletins from neighbouring towns carried over by travellers.
- Hindi and Assamese interface, a voice-note-first composer with a big mic button, and zero setup for sources.
- Low battery and storage footprint; off by default and switched on only during a shutdown.
- A way to verify that a sideloaded app file is genuine.
- Independent security audit, opinions from press-freedom groups, open source code, and real-world testing in crowds of hundreds.
- Everyday uses (melas, weddings, cricket) so that carrying the app is not a political statement.

## Deal-breakers

- Any account, phone number or persistent identifier that could link sources to him.
- Loss or silent alteration of his original footage, including the app touching the camera roll or recompressing originals.
- An app so conspicuous (Bluetooth-discoverable, or known as "the protest app") that carrying it marks him as a participant rather than a reporter.
- No independent audit, just "trust us".
- Draining the phone by afternoon or while idle.
- An English-only interface.
- A credible report that the app has been compromised (he would uninstall it from the main phone at least).

## Design implications

1. Add an opt-in "signed press post" mode alongside anonymous posting: a stable identity key the author chooses to reveal, verified in person by animated-QR key exchange, shown to recipients as "signed by known contact" (and never a self-declared name). Keep it strictly separate from anonymous mode so signing never leaks into anonymous posts.
2. Build a provenance manifest: a hash of the original at capture, a signature, and a clear "original" or "compressed copy, original held by sender" status that survives relay hops and Tor publication. Verification should work offline in the recipient's app or browser view.
3. Support media in a tiered way: a small preview or compressed clip sent first, the original queued and sent on request or when a high-bandwidth hub appears. Show honest progress and size and data-cost estimates, and never recompress or overwrite the original. Be explicit that video realistically moves only over a direct Wi-Fi link to one nearby phone or hub, or through a gateway with real bandwidth, not quickly across several Bluetooth hops; do not imply otherwise in the UI.
4. Make source protection the default: a per-clip export sheet with three big choices (time only, time + place, nothing) and optional face blur, plus a "protect source" label embedded in the file that shows a warning when the file is opened.
5. Keep app storage sandboxed: never read from or write to the camera roll without an explicit per-item action. Ship a plain-language "What BuzzChat stores on this phone" screen.
6. Replace the single emergency wipe with tiered, honest controls: wipe source and contact links, wipe chats and keys, keep media. Present the full wipe as a calm at-home option for volunteers, add plain-language notes on its legal implications, and make no claims beyond app-owned data.
7. Reduce conspicuousness: off by default, a one-tap "shutdown mode" with optional time limits, research into rotating identifiers within bitchat compatibility (the fixed service UUID remains visible to scanner apps today), and no BLE advertising when idle. Document detectability honestly, and promote everyday uses (melas, weddings, cricket) so the app is not "the protest app".
8. Redesign bulletin trust: drop raw "seen by N" counts, show confirmations from distinct nearby witnesses, verified medic or volunteer badges vouched for in person, a visible age that fades, an "unconfirmed" default, and a way to correct or retract. Rate-limit alarming categories.
9. Add a "news in" digest that carries bulletins from neighbouring areas, labelled by origin and age, for travellers moving between towns.
10. Localise into Hindi and Assamese (and others) from the start. Use a voice-first composer with a big mic button and make onboarding via QR two taps. Deliver the first message reliably, or show a clear "waiting for a nearby phone" status so the first experience does not silently fail.
11. Ensure sideload integrity: show the APK signing fingerprint in the app and on the sharing phone, and offer peer-to-peer verification by comparing a short code or QR.
12. Set strict power and footprint budgets (target negligible idle drain, a small install size, configurable media caps) and publish measured battery figures.
13. Before targeting journalists, commission an independent audit, publish a threat model that covers device seizure and BLE detection, seek review from press-freedom groups, and run field tests in crowds of hundreds.

## Interview transcript

### Q1. Who you are

I'm Farhan. I'm 35 and I split my time between Guwahati and Lucknow, depending on where the story is. I've been freelancing for about nine years. I started as a stringer for a Hindi daily, and now I sell to whoever pays: two digital outlets, sometimes a foreign wire desk, sometimes nobody. Most months I just about cover rent. My wife teaches at a private school, and her salary is the stable one, which my *ammi* (mother) reminds me of often. We have a daughter who is six.

I don't call myself a protester. I'm a reporter. But when you cover the same protests for years (citizenship law sit-ins, farmers, eviction drives, student agitations) people start to treat you as one side or the other. Some protesters think I'm a government informer because I film everything. Some officials think I'm an activist because I film them. I just think somebody should record what happened before ten WhatsApp versions replace it.

### Q2. Your phone(s)

My main phone is a mid-range Android, about ₹22,000, bought on EMI two years ago. It has 8 GB RAM and 128 GB storage, and the storage is always full. Video eats it. I offload to a laptop and two old hard drives, but in the field I'm always deleting something to make space for something else, and that scares me.

The backup is a ₹7,000 phone with a second SIM from a different operator. Nobody else uses the main phone, though my daughter grabs it for cartoons. The backup lives in the camera bag.

I carry a 20,000 mAh power bank. I keep the screen dim, and I turn off location history but switch GPS on for photos when I want a geotag. That's a contradiction I'll come back to. I'm on prepaid plans of about ₹300 to ₹350 a month on each SIM.

My apps are WhatsApp for everything, Signal for sources who'll install it (maybe one in five), Telegram for channels, a basic camera app, a timestamp camera app, Google Drive, and email. I type in English and Roman Hindi. For Assamese I mostly send voice notes because typing it is slow. Many of my sources only do voice notes.

### Q3. Your worst shutdown or network failure

December 2019 in Assam. Everyone remembers that the mobile internet was cut. What people don't remember is how disorienting it was hour by hour.

First day, evening: data died around dinner time. I was near a protest site. Calls still worked, so we did everything by phone and SMS, but SMS was patchy too.

Next morning: I had maybe 40 GB of footage across both phones, including police action, injured people and a lot of interviews. No way to send any of it. I went to a hotel that still had broadband, and the lobby was full of other journalists in the same situation. The Wi-Fi was so overloaded that one two-minute clip took over an hour. The desk in Delhi rang me every twenty minutes asking "*kab aayega?*" (when will it come?).

Third day: someone said a cyber café in another locality had a leased line. I went there. It had a queue and the owner was nervous. I uploaded compressed versions, and the quality was so bad the desk said it couldn't verify faces or signboards.

What broke: I couldn't confirm rumours, because there were stories of deaths in other districts that I couldn't check. I couldn't reach my stringers in smaller towns. And by the time my best footage reached Delhi on a pen drive with someone on a flight, it was two days old and nobody wanted it.

What it cost me: two exclusive stories, maybe ₹15,000 in lost fees, and something worse. One source's interview was published uncropped by an outlet I'd sent raw files to. He got calls. I still feel sick about that.

### Q4. Safety and fear

The biggest fear isn't being beaten up, although that has happened once. It's the phone being taken. If a policeman takes my main phone at a barricade, he gets every source's face and every voice note, and my WhatsApp tells him exactly who I talked to and when. I won't describe what I do about that in detail, and I'm not trying to hide from the law. I'm trying to protect people who talked to me in good faith. In general, I move sensitive material off the phone as soon as I can, and I don't save sources' names properly in my contacts.

Here's the contradiction. To prove footage is real, I want metadata: time, location, device. To protect the people in it, I want to strip all of that. Every day I'm deciding which one wins.

I also get harassed online. My name is Muslim, so when I report from UP the comments come quickly: "*jihadi patrakar*" (jihadi journalist), people posting my old photos, once someone posting my locality. My wife made me lock everything down. I trust maybe five people fully: two editors, one lawyer friend, my wife, and a fixer in Assam I've known for ten years.

### Q5. Information problems

During shutdowns, rumours travel faster than fact, even without internet, through word of mouth and calls. When the network comes back there's a flood of forwards: old videos from other states, Bangladesh videos passed off as Assam, morphed images. I spend half my time debunking.

Getting news out means getting footage to editors who can verify it. Editors are cautious now, and an unverified clip is worthless or even dangerous to them. Getting news in means knowing what's happening three kilometres away. Honestly, in a shutdown, the people around me know less than people in Delhi watching TV.

The other problem is that verification needs context. An editor wants to know who shot it, when, where, and on what device. If I can't answer, the story dies.

### Q6. Reaction to the BuzzChat concept

*[Concept explained: messages hop phone to phone over Bluetooth/Wi-Fi; join by QR code or browser link; posts marked "share to internet" go out through Tor via anyone nearby with internet; an expiring area bulletin board; encrypted private messages; QR-to-camera transfer; emergency wipe; plus the honest limits.]*

**What excites me:** the bulletin board, honestly. A noticeboard saying "first aid at this gate, water here, this road is closed" would kill a lot of rumours if people trusted it. And getting a post out as soon as anybody nearby has a connection, without queueing at a hotel, is exactly what I needed in 2019.

**What confuses me:** does it carry video? A text post saying "police lathi-charged at 4pm" is just one more claim. My job is evidence. If it only does text and small photos, it helps the crowd, but it doesn't help me get footage out. And when a post goes out "through Tor, author hidden", how does an editor know it's mine? Anonymity and authenticity pull in opposite directions. I'd want an option to sign a post with a stable press identity that I choose to reveal, plus a way to prove a file hasn't been altered since capture, without exposing my sources.

**What scares me:**
- "Anyone scanning Bluetooth can tell the app is running." At a barricade, that makes my phone the interesting one. For a reporter who needs to look neutral, that's a real cost.
- The emergency wipe. For me, wiping can look like destroying evidence and could create legal trouble. It also destroys my own footage, which is the whole point of being there. I'd want selective protection, so sources' details are protected and my footage is safe somewhere else. A wipe-everything button is something I would probably never press.
- Rumours could spread just as fast in a mesh. Who checks the bulletin board? If anyone can post "firing at the bridge", it becomes panic infrastructure.
- Installing an app file from a friend's phone. I'd worry about a tampered copy. How would I check it's genuine?

**What would stop me:** if it eats battery, if it's English-only, if it needs a crowd to work (I'm often alone in a small town), or if it gets a reputation as "the protest app", which would make carrying it a liability.

**What I'd need before I trust it:** an independent security audit I can point to, journalists' or press-freedom groups' opinions, the open-source code (I can't read it, but someone I trust can), a clear explanation of exactly what gets stored on my phone, and real testing in a crowd of a few hundred people.

**What I'd tell others:** "It's useful for the crowd, not magic. Don't put anything on it you wouldn't want read out in court." I'd recommend the bulletin board first and the rest later.

### Q7. Must-haves and deal-breakers

**Must-haves, in order:**
1. A way to prove authenticity: tamper-evident photos, video clips and timestamps that I can choose to sign, separate from anonymous posting.
2. Source protection by default: stripping faces or metadata on export should be one tap, and stated clearly.
3. Media support: at least compressed video, with the full file queued for later.
4. Hindi and Assamese interface and voice notes, because half my sources won't type.
5. Low battery drain and a low storage footprint.
6. Moderation or trust signals on the bulletin board, such as "seen by 30 nearby" or verified volunteer medics.

**Deal-breakers:**
1. Any account, phone number or identifier that could tie sources to me.
2. Loss or silent alteration of my original footage.
3. An app so conspicuous that carrying it marks me as a participant rather than a reporter.
4. No independent audit, just "trust us".
5. Draining my phone by afternoon. A dead phone is worse than any shutdown.

## Follow-up interview

### Follow-up 1. You said switching GPS on for photos while turning location history off was a contradiction you'd come back to. Could you walk me through the last time you had to decide in the moment whether a clip kept its location and time or lost them? What did you weigh, and how did it turn out?

Last winter there was an eviction drive in a district in Assam. Families were sitting on their belongings while bulldozers worked behind them. I filmed a wide shot of the machines and the signboard with GPS on, because I knew the administration would later say "that wasn't our site." Then an elderly woman wanted to speak. She was angry and named a local official. Before she started, I thought about it: if this clip carries exact coordinates and a timestamp, anyone holding the file can work out which hut she lives in. So I turned GPS off for her and kept only the time.

It half worked. The wide shot carried the story, and the desk could match the signboard and the machines to the location data. But my editor still asked, "How do we know she was at the same place?" I had to point out that the same shawl and the same blue tarpaulin appear in both clips. That was enough, but only just. I'm making these calls by instinct in about ten seconds, with a crowd pressing around me.

### Follow-up 2. When your source's uncropped interview was published, what part of that chain from you to the outlet felt out of your hands? Looking back, what would have had to be different, in the tools or in the agreements, for that not to happen?

Everything after the pen drive left my hands. In 2019 I was panicking about time, so I sent the raw folder, around forty files, with a note that said "blur faces on interviews 3 and 7." That note sat in a text file nobody opened. A junior producer on a night shift picked the strongest clip, and it was number 7. Nobody was malicious. There was just nothing in the file itself that said "don't use as is."

Two things should have been different. First, the agreement. Now I write it into the email in plain words: "Use of raw identifiable footage requires my written OK per clip." Even so, freelancers have no leverage. Second, the tool. The protection should travel inside the file and not in a separate note. If a clip is marked "source: protect", the receiving side should see a warning the moment they open it, or I should have sent only a blurred version, with the original staying with me until they ask. The default should have been safe. Instead, safety depended on a tired person reading a text file.

### Follow-up 3. Picture your editor in Delhi receiving a clip from you that was relayed through strangers' phones and Tor. What would they need to see or check before they treated it as yours and unaltered? How do they verify your material today, when you send it the normal way?

Today the checking is mostly about relationships, plus a bit of forensics. My editor knows my number and my voice, and I usually send a voice note: "Farhan here, this is from the protest at such-and-such place, shot at 4:10." They look at the metadata, match landmarks on a map, check the weather and shadows, and sometimes ask for the next clip in the sequence. A video verification person might compare it with other uploads.

Through strangers' phones they'd be suspicious by default, and they'd be right to be. They'd want to see that the clip is signed by a key they already have from me, something we exchanged in person, so they get a green "this is Farhan's key" and not just a name anyone could type. They'd want proof that the file hasn't changed since I signed it, even after hopping through ten phones. And they'd want to know whether it's the original or a compressed copy. If BuzzChat could show "signed by known contact, unaltered, compressed copy, original held by sender", my editor would treat it like a normal clip. Without that, it's just a forward.

### Follow-up 4. You said you're often alone in a small town, and that carrying something known as 'the protest app' would mark you. In those situations, what would make it worth having BuzzChat on your phone at all? At what point would you uninstall it or leave it off?

Alone in a small town, the mesh is mostly useless to me, because there's nobody to hop through. What would make it worth keeping is the moment the network goes down and I walk into a market or a bus stand where a few dozen people have it. Then one person with a working connection, maybe at a hotel or an office, could get my signed post out without me queueing anywhere. It would also be worth having if it gave me the "news in" part: a bulletin from the next town that someone carried over.

It would also help if it did ordinary things well, like offline messaging at a wedding or a mela, so that having it on my phone wasn't a statement. If half of Guwahati used it to coordinate cricket matches, I wouldn't worry.

I'd leave it switched off by default and turn it on only during a shutdown. If it got a reputation as the thing police look for, if it drained my battery while idle, or if there was a credible report of it being compromised, I'd uninstall it from the main phone at least.

### Follow-up 5. In 2019 you spent much of your time chasing rumours you couldn't confirm. Imagine the area bulletin board shows 'firing at the bridge' with 'seen by 30 nearby' next to it. What would you do with that post? Would that number make you more or less likely to believe it?

Honestly, the number would make me slightly more nervous, not more confident. "Seen by 30" means thirty phones received it, and that's not the same as thirty people witnessing something. In a crowd a rumour gets seen by thirty people in a minute. If the label said "confirmed by 3 people who were within a short distance" or "posted by a verified medic", that would mean something. Even then, three scared people can be wrong together. A loud tyre burst has caused panic before.

What would I do? I wouldn't repost it or report it. I'd try to call someone I know near that bridge, my fixer or a stringer. If calls worked, I'd ask, "What can you hear? What can you see?" If nobody could confirm it, I'd treat it as unverified and maybe walk closer with care, as a reporter would. I'd also want the post to show its age. A "firing" post from two hours ago that's still circulating is dangerous. Old posts should fade and say clearly "unconfirmed" until someone corrects them.

### Follow-up 6. Between the full storage, the daughter watching cartoons on the main phone, and the backup in the camera bag, how do you decide what lives on which phone? Where would something like BuzzChat fit into that, if anywhere?

It's messy, not a system. The main phone is my life: family WhatsApp, my wife, the school group for my daughter's class, banking, editors, and the best camera. That's why it's the phone I worry about most. The backup is the work-only phone: the second SIM, a few work contacts, Signal, and the timestamp camera. It has very little storage and a poorer camera, so I don't really use it as a camera. Mostly it's for calls when the main SIM's network is down, and for when the main phone is dead.

My daughter's cartoons are why I keep a separate profile for her on the main phone, a habit my wife pushed. Still, she has deleted a file once by accident.

BuzzChat would go on the backup first, honestly, because I'd be testing it and I don't fully trust it yet. But the backup's battery is weak and its camera is bad, so if BuzzChat is going to carry my footage, eventually it has to go on the main phone. That only happens once it has earned trust, and once I know exactly what it stores and where.

### Follow-up 7. Many of your sources only use voice notes, and fewer than one in five will install Signal. Thinking of one such source, what would it take for them to receive or send something through BuzzChat? What might stop them, apart from you?

I'm thinking of a man in his fifties, a small shopkeeper in a town in Upper Assam (I won't say more). He uses WhatsApp only for voice notes and for forwards from his nephew. He reads Assamese, a little Hindi, almost no English.

For him to use it: the whole thing in Assamese, a big microphone button, and no account, no OTP, nothing to set up. If I can show him a QR code and his phone just opens the app, and in two taps he can send me a voice note, he might try it. It has to look as plain as WhatsApp.

What would stop him? His nephew telling him "that's a dangerous app, don't keep it." Fear that the police will see it on his phone. Storage, because his phone is full of good-morning images. And cost: if it downloads anything heavy, he'll worry about data, even though it's offline. Also, he simply won't remember the app exists unless it's useful between shutdowns. And if the first voice note doesn't arrive, he'll never open it again.

### Follow-up 8. You said you would probably never press a wipe-everything button. Is there any situation, for you or for someone you work with, where a wipe would be the right thing? What would 'selective protection' actually cover, in your own words?

For me, almost never, because my footage is evidence and my livelihood. But for some people I work with it makes sense. A young student volunteer who coordinated water and first aid during a sit-in, with a phone full of chats with other volunteers and no footage they need, would lose nothing they care about. Same with a source who only messaged me once and now just wants it gone. For them, a wipe of the app's own data, done calmly at home and not in a panic at a barricade, is reasonable. I'm not talking about destroying anything a court has asked for. That's a different matter, and people should talk to a lawyer.

Selective protection, in my words, means separating three things. Source identities, meaning who told me what, should be protected first and hardest. My contacts and chat history in the app come second. My footage comes last: it should be safely copied somewhere I control, never deleted by the app, and never silently altered. The app should only ever remove its own messages and keys, never my camera roll. And it should tell me plainly what it can and can't erase, without promising more than it can do.
