> Simulated persona for design research. Not a real person; quotes are synthetic. Validate with real, consenting participants before relying on these findings.

He drops BuzzChat the moment it costs him orders, looks like a "protest app" at a checkpoint, or asks him to read paragraphs or recruit friends.

## Needs

- Relaying off by default, or a one-tap "receive only / shift mode", with the battery and data cost shown and no background drain while he rides.
- No phone number, name, face, contacts or location, and nothing that can be linked to his rider account, UPI or Aadhaar-linked number.
- A seal check that takes one glance (green or not green, the posting time, the same mark as the known page), with unsealed "official" notices visibly greyed out.
- A browser or QR entry that installs nothing and stays tiny on a full 64 GB phone and a 2G connection.
- A Telugu, Hindi and Urdu interface that also works for Dakhni speakers, with plain words and no protocol jargon.
- Short sealed voice notes (20–40 s) and small poster-style images as the main formats.
- A local needs board that takes partial pledges ("I can bring 3 by 5:45"). Replies should be anonymous and not reveal location, requests should close automatically when filled, a sealed "received" confirmation should follow, and requests should be verified to prevent pranks.
- Key continuity: the old movement key endorses a new one, so legitimacy survives the loss of Instagram or X.
- A neutral, everyday framing (a walkie-talkie for floods, power cuts and college groups) that is acceptable at home and unremarkable at a checkpoint.
- Trusted distribution (Play Store or a proper website), minimal permissions and a complete uninstall that his sister can check.
- An endorsement from the movement's own main page in a reel, not from a tech explainer.

## Deal-breakers

- The app makes his phone look suspicious at a checkpoint (an obvious "protest app" or a detectable Bluetooth beacon), or it is hard to remove completely.
- His phone dies during a shift, or relaying drains his battery or data and costs him orders.
- Anything that could connect back to his rider account, phone number, UPI identity or location.
- He has to convince ten friends to install it before it is useful.
- Installing an APK from someone's phone is the main way to get it (his sister and household see that as a virus risk).
- An English-only or jargon-heavy interface, or content that is mostly long text.

## Design implications

1. Make "receive-only / shift mode" the default for browser and low-end clients: no relaying and no advertising unless the user turns on "help relay now" with a timer and an estimated battery cost. Never relay in the background without a visible indicator.
2. Turn the radio off when the user isn't looking: Bluetooth advertising should stop when the app is closed or after a short idle timeout, so a checkpoint scan finds nothing on a phone that isn't in active use.
3. Present BuzzChat as a general offline "walkie-talkie" messenger for floods, power cuts and college groups, not as a protest app. Keep the app name, icon and launch screen neutral, and treat movement channels as content rather than as the app's identity.
4. Make the seal readable at a glance: a big green check with the movement's familiar mark and a relative posting time. Show unsigned posts that claim to be "official" or "cancelled" greyed out with a "not verified" label. Never require reading a key fingerprint.
5. Support key delegation and rotation, where the old movement key signs the new one, and show the endorsement in plain words ("Same people as before"). Signed status or heartbeat posts should prove the channel is still alive when Instagram is down.
6. Add short (under 40 s) voice notes, signed by the movement key, as a first-class format, compressed at Opus-like rates to fit mesh and 2G limits. Treat signed poster images as the main format for logistics and keep text secondary.
7. Localise fully into Telugu, Hindi and Urdu (with right-to-left support) and test with Dakhni speakers. Remove protocol words (Nostr, Tor, relay, mesh) from the interface: "Share to internet later, your name hidden" instead of "Publish to Nostr via Tor".
8. Volunteer template: split requests into units that people can pledge partially ("I can bring 3"), allow anonymous pledges without location, show a live remaining count, close automatically with "filled, thank you", send a sealed "received" acknowledgement, and require the seal on requests to block pranks.
9. Never require a phone number, contacts, photos or location permission. Show a plain-language permissions page that a family member can check. Make uninstall leave nothing behind, and offer a one-tap "clear all BuzzChat data" so deletion in a panic doesn't spill into personal files.
10. Make it easy to share out: export signed posters and voice notes to WhatsApp with the seal visibly embedded and checkable by scanning. That way BuzzChat content reaches the networks he actually uses, and Nostr isn't "shouting in an empty ground".
11. Ship the Android app through official channels (Play Store, F-Droid, a verified website with checksums). Present friend-to-friend APK sharing as a fallback for shutdowns only, with the integrity check explained in plain words.
12. Avoid identity cues that make communal profiling easier: random neutral default nicknames, no avatars by default, and reactions as a low-exposure way to take part.
13. Keep the browser client tiny and give it a clear "no hub nearby" state that explains what to expect instead of an empty feed, so a first scan at a poster doesn't feel broken.
14. Adoption depends on the movement announcing BuzzChat in its own reel as "our official channel during shutdowns". Give organisers short, localised, non-technical launch material and explainers on how to check the seal.

## Interview transcript

### Q1. Who you are

Imran. I'm 24 and I've been riding food delivery in Hyderabad for about three years. Before that I did two years of a B.Com at an evening college and left halfway when my father's auto-rickshaw loan got bad. Ammi (mother) does tailoring from home. My younger sister is in Inter (Class 11–12) and wants to do pharmacy. Her fees are the reason I don't take days off.

On a good day I do 10–11 hours and come home with maybe ₹700–900 after petrol. On a bad day it's less, and the app changes the incentive slabs whenever it feels like it. The bike EMI doesn't change.

I didn't join because of the cockroach thing at first. Honestly I thought it was a meme page, timepass. Then the NEET leak happened. My cousin's daughter had been preparing for two years and now she has to write it again. She stopped eating properly. That was when it stopped being a joke. When I watched reels of people sitting at Jantar Mantar, I thought, these people are saying what my whole gully (lane) says every day: you study, you pay, and still the paper leaks and there's no job. So after a shift a few times I went to the local solidarity sit-ins here, in my delivery jacket because I had no time to change. I stayed maybe one hour each time. I couldn't go to Delhi. Who will pay for the train, and who will ride my hours?

### Q2. Your phone and online life

It's a ₹9,000 Android, two years old. The battery is finished. By 3pm it's at 30% even with power saving, so I carry a cheap power bank. It has 64 GB of storage and it's always full, because WhatsApp forwards and the delivery app and maps eat everything. I clear WhatsApp media every week.

My plan is 1.5 GB per day at around ₹300 a month. Maps and the rider app use most of it. If I watch reels at lunch I'm on 2G speed by evening.

- **WhatsApp:** everything. Family group, rider union-type group, two or three movement forward groups.
- **Instagram:** reels only. I watch, I don't post. Sometimes I share to my status.
- **YouTube:** news clips in Hindi and Urdu.
- **Telegram:** one channel, for exam-leak news for my sister.
- **X, Discord, Signal:** nakko (no). I don't know anyone on Discord. Signal I've heard of, but nobody I know uses it.

I don't make memes. No time, and also I don't want my face anywhere. I forward things. That's my contribution, if you can call it that.

### Q3. The movement

From my side it was organised by reels and WhatsApp forwards. Somebody posts "Saturday 7pm, at such-and-such ground", it gets forwarded 50 times, and people come. It worked because it was funny. Uncles who never talk politics were forwarding cockroach stickers.

What broke:

- **The X account.** When it got blocked I only heard about it from a reel saying "they blocked it". I never used X anyway. The Instagram was fine.
- **Fake times and places.** In our forward group two different messages came for the same evening, with different places. One said it was cancelled. Nobody knew which was real, half the people went to one spot and half went home.
- **No network at the gathering.** When everyone is filming, the network dies. You can't even send "where are you" to your friend.
- **The Delhi internet cut.** That happened in Delhi, not here. I saw it in reels later.

There was a volunteer for water. People sent stuff by delivery apps. One time I was actually the rider delivering water bottles to a protest point, and I laughed. I was customer and protester at the same time.

### Q4. Safety and fear

My biggest fear is very simple: my rider ID gets blocked. If the company or police connect my face to a protest video and something happens to my account, I'm finished. No appeal, no human to talk to. Even now I'm scared the app will replace us with drones or some AI-routing thing. I don't need to give them an extra reason.

I ride through police checkpoints every night. Mostly they check papers and helmets. But in those weeks I thought, if they ask for my phone and open WhatsApp, what will they see? Forwards with the Education Minister's photo as a cockroach. Is that illegal? I don't know. Nobody knows what is illegal, and that's the fear. So yes, I deleted some groups and some videos. Not because I'd done anything, just because I was scared.

My father said straight out: "Tum log ka naam aayega, hum log ka ghar khatam" (your name gets out, our household is finished). My mother just cried. So I keep my face out of every camera and stand at the back.

Trolling I don't really get, because I don't post. But one guy from my area who posted his face got called "Pakistani" in the comments. Because of his name. You understand? For people with names like mine it's double.

### Q5. Information problems

The forward groups are full of rubbish. "Official notice: protest cancelled by police order", with some stamp that looks fake. AI photos of crowds, and you can't tell if it's from Delhi, Dhaka or last year. Someone forwarded a voice note "from the organisers", and later people said it was fake.

How do I verify? Honestly I don't. If three groups send the same thing I believe it a little more. If it comes from the main Instagram I believe it. That's it. I don't have time to check like journalists do. I'm on the bike.

### Q6. Reaction to BuzzChat

*(Interviewer explained the concept in plain words.)*

**Good:**

- **No number, no account.** That's the first thing I liked. My number is linked to everything, my Aadhaar (national ID), my bank, my rider ID. Something not tied to my number? Hau (yes), that matters.
- **The seal on official posts.** This is actually useful for me. If the real movement posts have a mark nobody can copy, then that fake "cancelled" notice dies. Solve that one problem and I'm interested.
- **The bulletin board with needs and shifts.** Like "need 20 water cases at 6pm". I could literally see that and think, I'm passing there anyway.
- **QR on a poster, opens in the browser.** Good, because I'm not installing one more app. My storage is full. I'm serious.

**Cringe, useless, or scary:**

- **Bluetooth needs a crowd.** I'm a man on a bike. Most of my day I'm alone on roads. So it's only for the protest spot itself, for one hour. Fine, but then it's a tool for an evening, not my daily app.
- **The battery.** If Bluetooth and Wi-Fi are always on and it's relaying other people's messages, my phone dies by 1pm and I lose orders. That's a deal-breaker, full stop.
- **Police can tell it's running.** You said anyone scanning can see that. So at a checkpoint, does having it on my phone make me look guilty? That scares me more than anything. If they open my phone and see a "protest app", that's worse than WhatsApp forwards. WhatsApp everybody has.
- **No reels.** Then my friends won't care. The movement grew on reels. Text posts and small photos, my rider friends won't open it.
- **Nostr and Tor.** I didn't understand those words, sorry. "Goes to the internet later, author hidden" I understand. But if nobody I know is on Nostr, who is reading it? It's like shouting in an empty ground.
- **"Install from a friend's phone."** My sister would say that's how you get viruses. People here don't trust sharing APKs anymore.

Would I use it? At a gathering, if a volunteer holds up a QR and says "official updates here, with the seal", yes, I'd scan it. Would I keep it? Maybe not. My friends would use it only if the internet actually gets cut. Until then WhatsApp wins, because everyone is already there.

What would make it spread: if the main movement page says "this is our official channel during shutdowns" in a reel. Not some tech guy explaining it. And it has to be in Telugu, Hindi and Urdu, not English only.

### Q7. Must-haves and deal-breakers

**Must-haves, in order:**

1. It must not kill my battery or eat my data. Let me switch relaying off.
2. No phone number, no name, no face needed.
3. A seal on official posts that I can check in one tap, so fake notices are obvious.
4. Works from a QR in the browser, nothing to install, small.
5. Telugu, Hindi and Urdu, with simple words and no "Nostr" type jargon.
6. A short local board for needs and timings that disappears after the event.

**Deal-breakers:**

1. It makes my phone look suspicious at a checkpoint, or it's hard to remove completely.
2. My phone dies during a shift.
3. Anything that could connect back to my rider account or my number.
4. It needs me to convince ten friends to install it first. I don't have that time.

Look, I'm not against it. I just have maybe ten free minutes a day. If it saves me from going to the wrong place because of a fake forward, that's worth something. If it costs me one day's orders, then it's not.

## Follow-up interview

### Follow-up 1. You said having a 'protest app' on your phone could feel worse at a checkpoint than WhatsApp forwards, because everybody has WhatsApp. Can you take me through one of those nights when you went and deleted groups and videos? What were you picturing would happen, and how did you feel afterwards?

It was after my last order, maybe 11:30. I was sitting on the bike outside a closed medical shop and finishing my chai. In the rider group someone wrote that at some naka (checkpoint) the police took a boy's phone and scrolled through it. Nobody knew which naka or which boy. It was just a rumour, but that was enough.

I started picturing it. A constable scrolls, finds the cockroach minister sticker and asks "ye kya hai?" Then he calls the senior. My name goes in some register. Abba comes to the station at 2am in his chappals. Then somehow the company finds out and my ID is gone.

So I left two forward groups and deleted the sit-in videos, even the funny stickers. While clearing I also deleted the photo of my sister's marks memo by mistake.

For ten minutes I felt relief. Then I felt like a thief, but I hadn't stolen anything. The next morning my uncle sent the same sticker to the family group with three laughing emojis. I didn't know whether to laugh or feel stupid.

What I want is something that looks normal, not something that looks hidden.

### Follow-up 2. Think back to the evening when two forwards gave different places and one said it was cancelled. What did you do, step by step, and what would have needed to be true for you to trust one of them while you were on the bike?

Around 5:30 I was at a signal and the first forward came: 7pm, at the ground near the old market. At 6 I was waiting outside a biryani place for an order, and a second one came with a different ground and the same time. Then a third: "Cancelled by police order," with a round stamp that looked like it was made on a phone.

I typed "?" in the group. Three people gave me three different answers. I called a friend from the rider group, but he was on a trip too and didn't pick up. I opened Instagram and the last post was from the morning, nothing new. By then it was 6:40, the app gave me a good incentive slab, and I took more orders and went home.

Later I found out it happened at the first place. It was small, maybe forty people. I felt like a fool.

To trust one on the bike, it needs to work in one look at a red light. The same mark as the real page, the time it was posted, and green or not green. And a "cancelled" message without the mark should look grey and dead straight away. I can't read paragraphs. I have ten seconds.

### Follow-up 3. You said you'd believe BuzzChat is the official channel if the main page said so in a reel. When the X account was blocked, you only heard about it through a reel. What would you do if that Instagram page itself disappeared or started saying something different?

Honestly? I'd be confused, like everybody. If it just disappeared, I'd assume it got blocked, and then I'd wait for some other reel to tell me so. That's how it works for me: someone tells me what happened in a reel.

If it started saying something strange, like "everyone go to this new place now" or "trust this new channel, ignore the old one", then I wouldn't move. Strange and urgent together means wait. I'd ask in the rider group, and I'd ask the people I've actually seen standing there, like the water volunteer. I trust faces I've met more than accounts.

This is where your seal thing matters, I think. If Instagram is gone but the posts on BuzzChat still have the same mark as before, then I know it's the same people. But if they need a new mark, the old mark should say "this new one is ours." Like when a known person writes a letter introducing someone new.

Without that, any page saying "we are the real ones now" just becomes one more forward.

### Follow-up 4. You were the rider delivering water to a protest point, customer and protester at the same time. If you had seen 'need 20 water cases at 6pm' on a board, what would you have done next, and is there anything about picking up or replying to it that would worry you?

First thought: twenty cases on a bike? Nakko, impossible. Maybe two or three on the back. So I'd want to reply "I can bring 3 by 5:45" without writing my name. Then the next guy brings his three, and so on.

What worries me:

- **Money.** Three cases is maybe ₹500–600. That's most of a day for me. If it's "please donate", I can't. If someone pays, how, without UPI showing my name?
- **The delivery app.** If it goes through the app as an order, my rider ID, the drop location and the customer name are all in their system forever. Last time it was just a job that came to me, but if I choose it, that's different.
- **Fake requests.** What if it's a prank and I reach there and nobody's there? I've lost an hour and maybe my incentive.
- **Ten people bringing water.** Once it's filled, the request should disappear, or say "done, thank you."
- **My reply.** Does my reply show where I am? It shouldn't.

If someone confirms "received" with the seal, I'd feel I actually did something. That's nice.

### Follow-up 5. When something from the movement does reach you and your rider friends, what form is it usually in: a reel, a voice note, a sticker, a photo with text? Which of those would you still open on 2G at the end of the day?

Mostly reels, forwarded on WhatsApp and not from Instagram directly. Then voice notes, often in Dakhni or Urdu, from somebody's cousin who "was there." Stickers, lots of stickers. For timings it's usually a photo with text, like a poster, a phone number blurred, place and time in big letters.

At night on 2G:

- **Reel:** forget it. The circle keeps spinning and I give up.
- **Voice note:** yes, if it's short, 20–40 seconds. Riders love voice notes. You put one earphone in while waiting at the restaurant counter. Not everyone reads well, and some of my friends read Telugu better than Hindi and English not at all.
- **Sticker:** yes, it's tiny, but it tells you nothing useful.
- **Photo with text:** yes, if it's small and from someone I know.
- **Long text:** nobody reads it. Sorry, but true.

So if BuzzChat wants riders, it should do small poster-type images and short voice notes in our language, both with that mark on them. Not long paragraphs, and not videos that need 4G.

### Follow-up 6. You mentioned a guy from your area being called 'Pakistani' in the comments because of his name, and said for people with names like yours it's double. How does that shape what you're willing to say or show, even in places where you don't use your real name?

Even without my name, I watch myself. The way I type gives me away: "Inshallah", "Khuda Hafiz", Urdu words. In mixed groups I've started writing "ok bye" instead. Nobody told me to do that. I just started.

I don't forward anything with a religion angle, even if it's true. The same forward from an Imran and from a Ravi gets read differently. If it's from me, suddenly it's "see, these people are behind the protest." I don't want the whole movement painted with my face.

So on something like BuzzChat, with a random name, I'd still pick something neutral. No profile photo, nothing that hints. I'd probably react more than write.

And I'm angry about it, honestly. Why should I have to shrink? My Hindu friends in the rider group say "arre, chhod, nobody cares." They mean well, but they don't get those comments. They don't stop at nakas with the same feeling.

So for me anonymity isn't only about police. It's also about not being the easy target in my own city.

### Follow-up 7. Your father said 'your name gets out, our household is finished.' If you put something like BuzzChat on your phone, would you tell anyone at home, your sister for example, who worries about viruses? What would they need to see or hear for it to be okay in the house?

My sister, yes. Abba and Ammi, probably not, at least not at first. My sister is the one who fixes everyone's phones at home, so she'd check it anyway.

She'd want to see:

- It comes from the Play Store or a proper website. No APK from somebody's phone.
- It doesn't ask for contacts, photos or location for no reason. She checks permissions like a teacher checks homework.
- When you uninstall it, it's actually gone, with nothing left behind.
- It's in Telugu or Hindi, so she can explain it to Ammi.

For my parents, it can't be "the protest app." If I say "it's for messages when the network goes down, like a walkie-talkie," that makes sense to them. During the floods a few years back, half our area had no signal for a day and Ammi couldn't reach Khala (aunt). If it's useful for that, for power cuts, or for my sister's college group, then it's just a normal app.

Ammi would believe it if a neighbour or someone at the masjid said they use it. Abba would believe it if it's on the TV news as safe. Until then, it stays between me and my sister.
