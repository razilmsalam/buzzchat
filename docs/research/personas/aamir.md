# Persona: Aamir (fictional)

> **Note**
> Simulated persona for design research. Not a real person; quotes are synthetic. Validate with real, consenting participants before relying on these findings.

A cautious Kashmiri student who wants an app that is "honestly ordinary". It should let his mother know he reached home, and it should never be the reason he, or his sister holding his phone, gets questioned.

## Needs

- An app that looks ordinary, with no disguise: a plain name, a neutral icon, no lock-screen content, no "N nearby users" counters, and a true one-sentence explanation ("family messages and local notices").
- Bluetooth and radio only when he turns them on, with zero background advertising when off.
- A short sync session he starts himself (e.g. ten minutes at the shop or a neighbour's gate) that ends with a clear summary like "3 sent, 5 received, nothing left to pass".
- Messages that wait on the phone for days rather than expiring within hours while they hop.
- A way to get short private messages ("we are okay", "exam postponed", "Nani needs medicine") to family outside the region.
- Short Kashmiri voice notes that record the same way as WhatsApp (press and hold).
- An interface in Urdu, Roman Urdu and Kashmiri, with pictures and icons for people with low literacy, and a contact's photo instead of a name.
- A one-tap mode for his father: carry messages with no settings to understand.
- A notice board with clear dates and expiry ("posted 3 hours ago, expires tonight"), wording that cannot be changed along the way, and separate "seen" and "I checked this myself" signals.
- Confirmation shown in terms of his own trust network ("2 of your contacts confirmed") rather than raw stranger counts.
- Slow file sharing for study PDFs, kept separate from news and chat, with who posted the file first and when.
- Disappearing messages by default, with timers he sets per conversation (longer for his mother's voice notes, short for everything else).
- No log of peers met nearby and no history of when or where Bluetooth was on.
- A separate app PIN, with nothing shown in notifications while it is locked (closed like a diary, not hidden).
- A one-screen explanation, also as a voice clip, of what someone holding the phone can and cannot see, including the honest limits.
- Relay mode that is off by default, chosen each time, time-limited, size- or contact-limited, stoppable at any moment, and showing a summary of what is being carried.
- Independent verification that the encryption works and that no logs are kept.
- Low battery, storage and RAM use on a three-year-old 4GB phone.
- Waiting states written to reassure ("Aamir's message is on the way"), not alarm.
- A one-time setup at home that never needs fixing, and support for each family member having their own number-free install.

## Deal-breakers

- Anything that marks him as a user to someone scanning nearby, unless he clearly controls it.
- Needing a phone number, email or any account.
- Large English-only screens full of security words his family will not understand.
- Battery drain while the app is off or in the background.
- Strangers being able to add him to groups.
- A disguise or fake app (e.g. a calculator) that looks like he was hiding something if discovered.
- A wipe that leaves an obviously blank phone in front of an examiner.
- Hearing even one story of someone being questioned because of the app.
- His mother not being able to use it.
- Relay mode that he cannot stop once it starts, that uses a relative's data without their knowledge, uses enough data to get noticed, or carries content completely blind.
- A large install size on an already-full phone.

## Design implications

1. Make radio use opt-in and session-based. Bluetooth/Wi-Fi advertising stays off until the user starts a "sync now" session with a visible countdown (e.g. 10 or 20 minutes), and stops automatically afterwards. There is no background advertising when the app is closed.
2. Reduce the app's fingerprint over Bluetooth. Investigate advertising that does not carry a stable BuzzChat-specific service identifier, and document honestly what an observer with a scanner can still see. Write that honest-limits statement in plain Urdu and Kashmiri.
3. Choose an honestly ordinary identity: a neutral name and icon, no security or political wording anywhere a person checking the phone would see it, no lock-screen previews by default, and no "N nearby users" or peer counters on launcher widgets or notifications. Do not ship a disguise mode.
4. Replace the dramatic wipe with data minimization by default: disappearing messages with per-conversation timers, notices that expire, no stored log of peers met, and no history of sync times or places. If a wipe stays, it should clear only BuzzChat data through a deliberate action and say plainly what it removed. It must never fill the app with decoy or fake content: when the app keeps little by default, an emptier app is simply its normal state, not a sudden blank.
5. Add an app-level PIN separate from the phone lock, with notifications that show nothing while locked. Treat shared phones as normal, and make it easy for each family member to have their own number-free identity on their own phone.
6. Show a sync completion summary in plain numbers ("3 sent, 5 received, nothing left to pass") before the user switches off. Let store-and-forward queues hold messages for days, with user-visible expiry.
7. Build a "carrier" mode for non-technical relatives (e.g. a shopkeeper father): one big button, no settings, carrying only encrypted traffic and public notices, with a clear on/off state.
8. Design an outbound private-message path for family outside the region (store-and-forward to a gateway, delivered encrypted to a known contact), not only public "share to internet" posts. Be honest about latency and risk.
9. Make internet relay off by default and approved per session, with a time limit, size and type limits ("text only", "from my contacts only"), an always-visible stop control, a data-use meter, and a summary of what is being carried (counts and types, plus content only for public posts). State plainly what the gateway's network, phone storage and post metadata do and do not reveal.
10. Notice board: show posted time and expiry prominently, sign content so wording cannot change in transit, show the original author's pseudonymous key and first-seen time, and offer separate "seen" and "I checked this myself" actions. Show confirmations from the user's own contacts first ("2 of your contacts confirmed"), down-weight or hide raw stranger counts, and never style counts as if the app endorses the notice.
11. Let notices be tagged by type (checkable practical info like "pharmacy open" versus unverifiable incident claims), with different trust cues for each.
12. Add short voice notes using WhatsApp-style press-and-hold recording, compressed heavily for slow Bluetooth hops, with display that suits Kashmiri speakers (contact photo, playback-first layout).
13. Add slow, resumable file transfer for documents (PDFs) in a separate "Files" area, opt-in per file, showing who first posted the file and the date, with a progress bar that survives interruptions. Never mix files into chat or notice syncs.
14. Localize fully into Urdu, Roman Urdu and Kashmiri, with icon- and picture-led layouts for low-literacy users. Before every OS permission prompt, explain it in the user's language, as a voice clip if needed, so the user is not caught off guard by English system dialogs.
15. Write reassuring status text in the family's language ("Aamir's message is on the way") instead of technical states like "pending" or "waiting for relay".
16. Provide one plain-language "If someone opens this app on your phone" screen with a voice version: what they can see, what they cannot, what has already expired, and an honest note that an unlocked, seized phone cannot be fully protected.
17. Block unsolicited adds and DMs by default. Groups and private chats should need mutual QR exchange or explicit acceptance from known contacts. Area channels should be read-only unless the user chooses to post.
18. Set strict budgets for app size, RAM and battery, and test on a three-year-old 4GB Android with full storage. Cap cache size and auto-prune expired content.
19. Publish independent security audits and a no-logs design summary in Urdu. Make the shared app file verifiable (e.g. a short fingerprint people can compare) to address fears of pen-drive-style malware.
20. Position the product honestly: it is for family messages and local notices, not invisibility, and it works best in dense neighbourhoods and short gatherings, not in full curfew isolation.

## Interview transcript

### Q1. Who you are

My name is Aamir. I'm 24 and doing my postgraduate degree in Srinagar. I live with my *mouj* (mother), my father, who has a small shop, my grandmother, and my younger sister, who is in Class 11. My elder brother is studying engineering outside the state, and my cousin is in Delhi. Most days go like this: university, the library if there is electricity, tuition classes I teach in the evening for pocket money, and then home.

I'll be honest: I'm not someone who stands in front of a crowd. People here learn early that being visible has a cost. What I do is quieter. During the lockdowns I helped run an informal list of students stuck outside Kashmir. We collected names so families could know who was safe. Last winter I volunteered with a group delivering medicines to elderly people when the roads were bad. I sign petitions about internet restoration and student rights. If you call that protest, then I protest. Mostly I want to finish my degree, and I don't want my sister to grow up thinking silence is normal.

### Q2. Your phone(s)

It's a budget Android, a Redmi-type phone that cost about ₹10,500 three years ago. It has 4GB RAM and 64GB storage, and storage is always full. WhatsApp media alone is 9GB, and I keep getting the "storage almost full" warning. When I install anything I have to delete something first, so app size matters to me.

My sister borrows it for online classes when her phone is with my father. The battery is weak now. I carry a cheap power bank, but power cuts in winter can be long, so I keep the screen dim and turn off Bluetooth and location. I'm on prepaid at around ₹300 a month, which is fine when 4G works and useless when it doesn't.

I rely on WhatsApp, Telegram for study PDFs, YouTube lectures, Google Classroom, Gmail, and two or three VPN apps, because one always stops working. I type English and Roman Urdu. My mother sends voice notes in Kashmiri because typing is hard for her. My grandmother only takes calls.

### Q3. Your worst shutdown or network failure

August 2019. The first day I didn't understand. I woke up and there was no signal at all, and the landline was dead too. By afternoon people were standing at the gate asking each other, *"Kya hua? Koi khabar?"* (What happened? Any news?). Nobody knew anything.

The first week, the only way to reach someone was to walk to their house, and walking wasn't always possible. My brother was outside and we had no way to tell him we were fine. Later he told me he had called every number for days, and then someone gave him a number at an official office where you could wait in line to make a call.

After that it went by months, not hours. We gathered news at the mosque, the shop and the hospital. We heard rumours that someone was taken and rumours that the phones were coming back on a date, and none of it was true. When the government internet facilitation centres opened, I queued for hours to submit one exam form. You get a few minutes. The person behind you is reading your screen.

Then came the long 2G period. 2G is a kind of torture. A WhatsApp text goes through, a photo takes ten minutes, a video lecture never loads, and you can't open Google Classroom at all. I lost almost a full year of proper study. I applied for one scholarship late because the email confirmation never arrived. Nobody ever counts that cost: thousands of small missed deadlines.

The workarounds were things like walking to a relative's house that had broadband for an hour, and passing pen drives with lecture videos between friends. That's how I studied that winter. Honestly, the pen drive was the real network.

### Q4. Safety and fear

*Khauf* (fear) is in the background of everything. Phones get checked, sometimes on the road and sometimes not. You don't know who is looking, or what they're looking for. A random forwarded message, a photo, a group name, someone else's post in a group you forgot you were in: anything can become a problem. So I delete things, and I leave groups. I don't keep news videos. I don't save politically loud people as contacts with their full names. Before travelling I go through my phone the way you check your pockets.

Informers are the other fear. You can't know who in a group is just curious and who is reporting. So I trust maybe five people, and they're family and two old school friends. I don't trust big groups. I barely trust my own phone.

For girls it's worse. My classmates have had their photos stolen and posted with nasty captions. Being a Kashmiri Muslim online also means people outside call you names the minute you say anything at all. So most of us just don't post.

### Q5. Information problems

When the internet is off, rumours fill the space. When it's on, fake videos fill it. I've seen the same video circulated as three different incidents in three different years.

How do I verify? I wait. I check if two people I personally know confirm it. I look at whether the source is someone with a name and a reputation. Mostly I don't forward anything. People call that cowardly. I call it *sabr* (patience).

Getting news out is the harder problem. When it's shut down, my brother and cousin know nothing except what TV says, and TV either says nothing or says everything is normal. I just want them to know *we are okay, the exam is postponed, Nani needs her medicine from Delhi.* Getting news in matters too: university notices, exam dates, which hospital has oxygen, when the curfew relaxes. Ordinary things.

### Q6. Reaction to the BuzzChat concept

*[Interviewer explained: messages hop phone-to-phone over Bluetooth/Wi-Fi with no internet; you join by QR code or browser link with no account or number; the app file can be shared from a friend's phone; "share to internet" posts go out through Tor when someone nearby has internet; an expiring area notice board; encrypted private messages; animated QR codes screen-to-camera; an emergency wipe button. Limits: short range, jammers block it, a browser-only phone needs a hub, and anyone scanning Bluetooth can see the app is running.]*

**What excites me:** The notice board. If it existed in 2019, my whole neighbourhood could have known the exam was postponed, which pharmacy was open, and when the relaxation hours were. Expiring notices are smart, because old rumours keep circulating forever. No phone number is also good, because here everything is tied to your SIM and your Aadhaar. Sharing the APK from a friend's phone makes sense too. We already did that with pen drives.

**What confuses me:** The "share to internet" part. Who is the person with the internet? If my post passes through his phone, is *he* in trouble? And if I'm the one with internet, do I become the gateway for everyone's posts without knowing what they are? I'd need that explained very simply. Also, does the browser version work on 2G, or with no data at all?

**What scares me:** You said anyone scanning Bluetooth can see the app is running. *Khodayo* (oh God). That's the biggest problem for me. In my situation, the app being found on my phone is itself the risk. The same goes for the wipe button. If someone sees me press something and the phone goes blank, that looks worse than whatever was in it. A wiped phone invites questions. I'd rather have a version that looks boring.

Also, Bluetooth range of tens of metres needs a crowd. In my area, during the worst times, there *was* no crowd. People stayed home. So mesh works on a protest day and fails in a curfew, and curfew is exactly when we need it. Maybe it works in a mohalla (neighbourhood) where houses are close. I'm not sure.

**What would stop me:** If it drains my battery, if it's big, if it's only in English, or if my mother can't use it. And if I hear even one story of someone being questioned because of it.

**What I'd need before trusting it:** Someone I personally know using it first. A clear explanation in Urdu of what is stored on my phone and for how long. Independent people, not the developers, saying the encryption is real. And proof that it doesn't quietly keep logs.

**What I'd tell others:** Probably "useful for family and notices, but don't treat it as invisible." I wouldn't promote it loudly. And it still doesn't solve my studies. I can't stream a lecture over Bluetooth hops. Maybe it could pass PDFs slowly, like the pen drive did?

### Q7. Must-haves and deal-breakers

**Must-haves, in order:**
1. **Does not look suspicious.** An ordinary-looking app, a neutral name and icon, and Bluetooth only when I turn it on.
2. **Family messages and voice notes.** Short Kashmiri voice notes for my mother, so it isn't text only.
3. **Urdu and Roman Urdu interface**, with pictures for people who can't read well.
4. **Light on battery and storage.** It should run on a three-year-old phone with 4GB RAM.
5. **The notice board**, with expiry and a way to tell how many people have seen or confirmed a notice.
6. **Slow file sharing** for study PDFs, even if it takes an hour.
7. **A simple explanation of what happens if my phone is taken.**

**Deal-breakers:**
1. Anything that marks me as a user to someone scanning nearby, without my clear control.
2. Needing a phone number, email or any account.
3. Big English-only screens full of security words my family won't understand.
4. Battery drain while it's off or in the background.
5. Unknown people being able to add me to groups.

*Akhir mein* (in the end), I don't need a hero app. I need something that lets my mother know I reached home, and doesn't make reaching home harder.

## Follow-up interview

### Follow-up 1. You said the app being found on your phone is itself the risk, and that you would prefer a version that looks boring. Can you walk me through what a phone check has looked like for you or people you know: what gets looked at, for how long, and what would make an app stand out or go unnoticed? Please share only what you are comfortable with.

I'll keep it general. It has happened to me twice. It's usually short, a minute or two, but in that minute your heart is in your mouth. They want it unlocked. They open WhatsApp first and scroll through the chat list and group names. Then the gallery. Sometimes they ask, "What is this app?" about something on the home screen.

One of my friends got held up longer because of a group name he hadn't opened in months. Nobody read the messages. The name was enough. So what stands out is anything you can't explain in one sentence: a strange icon, a name that sounds political or like "security," a notification that pops up while they're holding the phone.

I don't want an app that lies or pretends to be a calculator. If that gets discovered it's much worse, because then you look like you were hiding something. What I want is an app that is honestly ordinary: a plain name, no dramatic icon, no pop-ups on the lock screen, and nothing on the home screen like "17 nearby users." If they ask, I should be able to say "it's for family messages and local notices," and that should be true.

### Follow-up 2. Earlier you described keeping Bluetooth and location off to save battery and to stay safe, and you also said mesh needs a crowd that did not exist during curfew. If messages could only move when you chose to switch Bluetooth on, for example for a few minutes at a neighbour's gate, how would that fit, or not fit, into how your family actually passed news in 2019?

Actually, this fits better than "always on." In 2019 news moved in short meetings. During relaxation hours my father opened the shop for an hour, and people came for milk and bread and exchanged news. My mother talked to the neighbour over the wall. Friday at the mosque. Someone back from the hospital told the whole lane.

So if I switch Bluetooth on for ten minutes at the shop, my phone collects what's waiting and gives what I have. Then I switch it off and walk home. It's the same as the pen drive, just faster.

What worries me: Will it tell me clearly that everything has finished passing before I turn it off? A spinning circle won't do. I need "3 messages sent, 5 received." Will messages wait on my phone patiently for days, not expire after one hour? And the shop is where my father is, not me. Could his phone carry the messages without him needing to understand anything? He would do one button, not settings.

It won't help the house at the end of the lane where nobody goes out. Someone still has to walk there.

### Follow-up 3. You said the pen drive was the real network. What made people trust a pen drive passed between friends, and what, if anything, went wrong with it: wrong files, outdated notices, files that worried someone to carry?

We trusted it because of the hand it came from. A pen drive from my classmate Faisal — a made-up name, but someone like that — came with a person. I could ask, "Where did you get this lecture?" and he'd say, "From the professor's nephew, who downloaded it in Jammu." So there was a chain of faces.

Things did go wrong. Files had names like "new folder (3)," and nobody knew which semester they belonged to. One time an exam timetable went around that was from the previous year, and two people in my class went to the wrong exam centre. Viruses came too. One drive filled my laptop with shortcut files, and I lost assignments.

And yes, some drives had things mixed in that someone else had put there: news videos, forwarded clips. You'd be carrying a drive for your engineering notes and not know what else was on it. That worried people. Some of us started using separate drives only for study.

So for BuzzChat I'd want to know who first posted something and when, the date shown clearly, and file sharing kept separate from everything else. I want to carry only what I chose to carry.

### Follow-up 4. On the notice board, you asked for a way to see how many people have seen or confirmed a notice, but you also said you only trust about five people and rely on two people you personally know to verify something. How would you decide whether a notice from strangers in your area was true? What would a confirmation count need to show for you to believe it, and what could make it misleading?

You caught a contradiction, and it's real. A number by itself means nothing to me. "40 people confirmed" could be one person with forty phones, or forty people who just pressed the button because everybody else did. Rumours here already have big numbers behind them.

What would help: if the count showed me that someone *I* know confirmed it. "2 of your contacts confirmed" matters more than "40 strangers confirmed." Also, what kind of notice is it? "Chemist near the bridge is open" is something I can check by walking there. "Something happened in X area" I won't believe from any count.

I'd also want the age clearly shown: "posted 3 hours ago, expires tonight." And the original wording should stay unchanged as it's passed along, so nobody adds words in the middle.

What could make it misleading: people confirming things they didn't see, organised groups pushing one notice, or the count looking official, as if the app itself is saying it's true. The app should never look like it is vouching for anything. Maybe "seen by" and "I checked this myself" should be separate buttons.

### Follow-up 5. About the share-to-internet relay, you asked whether the person with internet is in trouble if your post passes through them. If you were that person, what would you need to know or control before your phone carried other people's posts, and what would make you refuse?

If I had internet, even a slow broadband at my uncle's place, I'd need to know three things in simple words. First: can anyone looking at my connection see what I'm sending, or who wrote it? Second: is anything stored on my phone after it's sent, or is it gone? Third: does my name or anything about my phone get attached to the post?

Control-wise, I want it off by default, and turning it on must be my decision every time, not a setting I enabled once in March and forgot about. I'd want a limit, like "only small text posts" or "only from my contacts," and a clear end, like "on for 20 minutes."

What would make me refuse: if I couldn't stop it once it started, if it used my uncle's data without him knowing, which is his connection and his risk, or if it used so much data that someone noticed. Honestly, also if I couldn't see *anything* about what I'm carrying. I know that's the point of encryption, but blind carrying feels like the pen drive with unknown files. Maybe at least show "3 text posts, 1 image, posted publicly." Public posts aren't secret anyway.

### Follow-up 6. You said a wiped phone invites questions and that pressing the wipe button in front of someone looks worse than what is on the phone. What would 'a simple explanation of what happens if my phone is taken' need to say for you and your family? And is there anything the app could do with your data, or avoid doing, that would make you feel less exposed in that situation?

The explanation should be one screen, in Urdu and Kashmiri, maybe a short voice clip for my mother. It should say plainly: "If someone opens this app on your phone, they can see X. They cannot see Y. Messages older than Z are already gone." No words like "forward secrecy." Just what someone holding my phone would see.

Also be honest: "If your phone is unlocked and taken, the app cannot protect you fully." I'd rather know the truth than feel falsely safe.

What would make me feel less exposed is the app keeping less in the first place. If messages disappear by themselves after a day or a week, there is nothing to wipe in a hurry. Notices expire. No list of people I met nearby. No history of where or when my Bluetooth was on. If the app is quietly empty most of the time, I don't need a dramatic button.

I'd want to choose the timer myself. For family I might keep voice notes a few days, because my mother's voice notes matter to me. For anything else, short. Boring and empty is safer than full and then suddenly blank.

### Follow-up 7. Your mother sends Kashmiri voice notes and your grandmother only takes calls. Imagine your mother receiving a message on BuzzChat without you there to help. What do you picture going wrong first, and who in the family would end up setting it up or fixing it?

First problem: she won't know a message came. She knows the WhatsApp sound. A new app with a different sound, or no sound because Bluetooth is off, and she'll never open it. Or she'll see some English pop-up like "Enable nearby devices permission" and press "Deny" because it sounds dangerous. Then she'll call it broken.

Second: she'll press record, and if it stops when she lifts her finger she won't understand. WhatsApp taught her one way. If BuzzChat records differently, her message gets cut after two words.

Third: if a message says "pending" or "waiting for relay," she'll get scared that something is wrong with me.

Who fixes it? My sister, honestly. She's 16 and faster with phones than me. When she's not there, it's me over the phone, which won't work if there's no network. So the setup has to happen once, at home, and never need fixing. Big buttons, her own language, a photo of me next to my messages instead of a name. And a message like "Aamir's message is on the way" in Kashmiri, so waiting doesn't feel like danger.

My grandmother won't use it. Someone will read messages to her. That's fine.

### Follow-up 8. You mentioned that your sister borrows your phone for online classes. How does sharing a phone change what you would be willing to keep on it, or what you would worry about if BuzzChat were installed?

It changes a lot. When she has my phone, she also has whatever is on it, and anything that happens to it. If she's on the road with my phone during a check, then the question is put to her, a schoolgirl, about my apps. That is my biggest worry. I would never want her answering for something I did.

So anything sensitive can't just sit open on the home screen. I'd want BuzzChat to need its own PIN, separate from the phone lock, and to show nothing in notifications when it's locked. Not hidden or disguised, just closed, the way a diary is closed.

There's also normal privacy. She's my little sister, and I don't want her reading my friends' messages any more than she wants me reading hers. And she might reply to a notice without understanding, or forward something to her class group.

Maybe the best design is the opposite: let her have her own BuzzChat on her own phone, with no number needed, so the family isn't all on one device. Sharing a phone is normal here, so the app should assume it, not be surprised by it.
