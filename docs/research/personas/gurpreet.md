# Persona: Gurpreet Kaur (fictional)

> **Note:** Simulated persona for design research. Not a real person; quotes are synthetic. Validate with real, consenting participants before relying on these findings.

**In one line:** BuzzChat could be a waterproof notice board inside the camp for a langar volunteer, but only if it works in Gurmukhi and by voice, barely uses battery, shows vouched committee labels, and has a wipe that clears only the app on a shared phone. As described it is English, text-first and needs help to set up, so she would quietly stop using it within a week.

Gurpreet Kaur (fictional), 52, farms about four acres of wheat and paddy in rural Punjab and volunteers for a farmer union. Over several months she stayed at a protest camp on Delhi's border in shifts, where she ran langar logistics: rations, milk supply and cooking and washing rotas. She does not call herself a leader. Her trust is local and face-to-face: her village, the gurdwara committee, and the women she cooked with. She judges rumours by asking "who saw it with their own eyes?" Her biggest fear is not that her messages will be read. It is that her words will be tied to her name and land on someone's list or in an edited smear video. When an app fails her she does not complain. She just stops opening it.

## Needs

- Gurmukhi Punjabi on every button, menu, confirmation and warning, with no English prompts in her core flows
- Voice notes as the main way to send and receive, with a clear sign that other women actually received hers
- Very low battery use, stated in plain words (for example, "about X% per day"), and the phone must not get hot
- Small storage footprint, with notices cleaned up automatically after they expire
- Notices from known groups (such as the langar committee) that look clearly different from posts by unknown people
- Committee status granted in person by someone already trusted, not claimed by typing a name
- A way to post under a group label ("langar committee") without her personal name or identity attached
- Notices that expire on their own after their shift or time
- Notices that stay in the camp and are held by many phones, not stuck on one person's phone
- A quick way for the committee to mark a rumour "Checked" or "False" with its label
- Rough headcount or arrival numbers and a single shared rota, so food and shifts can be planned
- Store-and-forward via a courier: messages ride out with someone travelling to signal, and replies come back with them
- A clear on-screen status for each post: stayed in the camp, or reached the outside
- One-time setup by a trusted local volunteer, after which she just opens the app
- A wipe that clears only BuzzChat data, explains in Punjabi what will and won't be removed, and is hard to trigger by accident
- Honest, plain explanation of risks (Bluetooth visibility, reach) without scaring or hiding, so each person can decide
- Something useful posted every day in the first week, and a feed that isn't flooded with jokes and speeches

## Deal-breakers

- English-only menus, or an app that needs typing
- Draining the battery, making the phone hot, or filling the already full storage
- An emergency wipe that deletes anything beyond BuzzChat on the shared phone, or that can be pressed by accident (wet or floury hands, phone in her dupatta)
- Anyone outside her circle (strangers, people making smear videos, officials) being able to see that she personally wrote something
- Anything that feels like a trick: an app file from unknown people, or a link or QR code she can't check
- Being introduced by a stranger with a QR code instead of by people she already knows, such as the sound-system volunteers
- An empty, silent feed, or one full of jokes, speeches and forwards

## Design implications

1. Ship Gurmukhi Punjabi as a first-class language, including every safety warning and wipe confirmation. Treat any English string in a core flow as a release blocker for this group.
2. Make voice the primary input. Use push-to-talk voice notes with heavy compression, voice playback of notices, and a delivery receipt from the people in her own group (for example "the langar women received it"), not a raw "heard by N nearby" count, which the synthesis warns makes rumours look credible. Measure the Bluetooth cost of audio and set limits on length and how far it hops.
3. Publish a battery budget and stick to it. Offer a low-power mode (scan in bursts, show the board on demand) and show estimated daily use in plain Punjabi. Test on 3-year-old mid-range phones with a full disk.
4. Cap storage use and delete expired notices and relayed traffic automatically. Show how much space the app uses on its home screen so her son doesn't delete it.
5. Add group identities that someone with existing trust grants in person, for example a committee key handed over by scanning face to face, with a visible badge and colour. Show unverified posts as "Unknown sender: nobody has confirmed" rather than hiding them.
6. Let people post under a group label so notices show "Langar committee", not a personal handle. Avoid storing per-author metadata that could be screenshotted or forensically tied to her.
7. Add a "Checked / False" response that verified groups can attach to a rumour post, shown wherever that rumour is displayed.
8. Build light coordination templates for the board: shift and rota notices with expiry, a quick "how many coming / bringing what" count, and "X is finished, bring from store tent" notices.
9. Keep board notices replicated across many devices in the area so night updates survive when any one phone is asleep or leaves.
10. Design store-and-forward for couriers in both directions: outgoing village messages leave with a courier who reaches signal, and replies come back with them. Show per-post status: "Still in camp", "Sent out with courier", "Reached outside".
11. Keep reach claims honest in onboarding and the UI. Say plainly that it works inside the camp and does not reach home unless someone carries it or has internet.
12. Plan for assisted setup. Have a trusted volunteer set it up once and pin the camp board to the home screen. Use no QR scanning in her daily flow.
13. Give a way to verify sideloaded app files that people can understand, for example a short spoken or visual fingerprint read out by known volunteers. Prefer distribution through trusted local volunteers over random sharing.
14. Limit the wipe to BuzzChat data only. Show a Punjabi confirmation saying "camp messages only, your photos and WhatsApp are safe", and require a deliberate gesture such as hold plus slide so it can't fire from a pocket or wet hands.
15. Explain Bluetooth detectability calmly in Punjabi with an analogy. Let users switch radio off and on easily, and consider a mode that is visible only while the app is open.
16. Keep public notices quiet by default: no notifications at night unless a verified group posts something urgent, and filters that push jokes and speeches below committee notices.
17. Plan the first week: seed the board with real daily operational notices from the committee so it is never empty. Watch for silent drop-off, because users won't complain.

## Interview transcript

### Q1. Who you are

Sat Sri Akal ji. My name is Gurpreet Kaur, I am 52. We have about four acres in our *pind* (village) in Punjab, mostly wheat and paddy, and some fodder for two buffaloes. My husband has bad knees now, so my son and I do most of the *kheti* (farming). My daughter is married and lives near Ludhiana.

I am not a leader. I am a volunteer. During the long protest at the Delhi border I spent many months at the camp, going home and coming back in turns with other women from our village. My *sewa* (service) was the langar: how much atta, how much dal, who brings milk from which village, who cooks the morning shift and who washes the big pots at night. It sounds small, but if the langar stops, people go home. We were there because the farm laws frightened us. Small farmers like us live from one crop to the next. If the price is not secure, we lose the land, and land is not just money for us. It is our *izzat* (dignity).

### Q2. Your phone(s)

It is one phone and two people. My son bought it maybe three years ago, a Samsung or Redmi type, around 12,000 rupees. I honestly forget which. Android. The storage is always full. It says "storage almost full" every day because WhatsApp groups send so many videos, *gurbani* (hymns) clips, and news. My son deletes things and I get angry because sometimes he deletes the rota photos.

During the day the phone is mostly with me. At night he takes it. Battery is the big problem. At the camp we charged phones from a tractor battery or a solar panel some boys put up, and there was always a line. I keep brightness low and turn off data when I am not using it. The plan is a prepaid pack, about 300 rupees a month, with 1.5 GB a day. It finishes fast with YouTube.

My apps are WhatsApp, YouTube, the phone dialer, and a UPI app my son set up. I read Gurmukhi well. English I read slowly, word by word. I don't type much. I send voice notes, maybe 90 percent of the time. When I do type, I use Punjabi with the keyboard my granddaughter showed me. If an app shows only English menus, I press buttons and hope.

### Q3. Your worst shutdown or network failure

It was the end of January 2021, after the trouble on Republic Day. They suspended internet around the border camps. I remember it hour by hour because it was a langar problem.

On the first evening, WhatsApp just stopped. Messages went out with one tick and stayed one tick. Calls worked sometimes, but the network was very crowded and calls dropped.

By the next morning we did not know how many people were coming. Usually the village groups send a voice note like "two trolleys coming, 40 people, bringing 5 quintal atta". Nothing came, so we cooked by guessing. The first day we cooked too much and food was wasted, which hurts me even now. The next day we cooked too little because people came back worried and in big numbers.

We also had no news. Rumours started at once: that the camp would be cleared tonight, that the water would be cut, that a certain group had left. Women were crying because they couldn't reach their sons who had gone to the city.

Here is what we did instead. We wrote rotas on paper and pinned them on the langar tent. Boys went on motorcycles to the next camp with written notes. Some people drove a few kilometres away where the signal worked, sent messages, and came back. We used the stage announcements a lot. Someone with a microphone is still the best internet.

What did it cost us? Wasted food, one volunteer shift that never came because they didn't know the change, and a lot of fear. My son in the village did not hear from me for almost two days. He thought I was hurt.

### Q4. Safety and fear

I am not a criminal, so why should I be afraid? But I am afraid, a little. At the camps there were always unknown people taking videos. Some were journalists, some we didn't know. We heard about people's phones being checked at *nakas* (checkpoints), but I never saw it myself.

What scares me more is online. Some videos of our women were cut and shared with ugly words, calling us paid, calling us *Khalistani*, saying Sikh women were brought on buses for money. That hurts. My daughter's in-laws saw one such video and asked questions.

What I delete: I don't delete much, to be honest. I don't know how. My son deletes the group chats sometimes when he is worried. Because we share the phone, he can see everything I have, and I can see his. That is normal for us, but it means nothing on the phone is really "mine".

Who do I trust? People from my village, our *gurdwara* (Sikh temple) committee, and women I cooked with for months. I don't trust a new number just because it says "Waheguru" in the profile.

### Q5. Information problems

Rumours were worse than the cold. A voice note would come saying "Police coming at 2 a.m., everyone wake up", and nobody knew who first said it. My way of checking is simple. I ask: who saw it with their own eyes? If nobody, I wait. But many people forward first and ask later.

Getting news out was hard. Our side was not always shown on TV. Young boys made YouTube videos, and we depended on them. Getting news in, we depended on the stage and on people coming from the village.

What I want is to know who is saying it. Not the person's name, maybe, but whether it is from the langar committee or from some unknown person.

### Q6. Reaction to the BuzzChat concept

*(Interviewer explained the concept in Punjabi.)*

So phones talk to each other without the tower? If that worked during those two days in January, it would have saved food. The bulletin board idea I like very much. "Langar morning shift, 6 a.m., need 10 people for *roti* (bread)", and it disappears after the shift. That is exactly a paper notice that does not get wet. I like that it needs no phone number, so my number is not going into another group of 500 strangers.

What confuses me is "hops phone to phone". A crowd of tens of metres, okay, the camp was a crowd. But my son's village is 300 km away, so it won't reach him. Only "share to internet" would do that, and someone nearby must have internet. Then what is the use in a full shutdown? It will help *inside* the camp, not outside. You should say that clearly.

QR codes and links I can't do alone. My son or granddaughter would have to do it. "Install the app file from a friend's phone" sounds like the kind of thing my son warns me about: "*Bebe* (mother), don't install anything people send, it is fraud." How do I know the file from a friend is the real one and not someone's *jhooth* (lie)?

What scares me is the line "anyone scanning Bluetooth can tell the app is running". So if police or someone checks, they know? And the emergency wipe. What if I press it by mistake and lose my son's photos? It is his phone also.

What would stop me: if it is in English only, if it needs typing, and if it eats battery. If my phone dies at noon, the langar has no phone.

Before I trust it, I need to hear about it from someone I know, like the young volunteers who managed our sound system, not from a stranger with a QR code. And I need to see it work once, in our camp, with our people.

What would I tell others? "It is like a *dhol* (drum) announcement that goes phone to phone. Good for inside the camp. Don't think it reaches home."

### Q7. Must-haves and deal-breakers

**Must-haves, in order:**
1. Voice notes, and Punjabi in Gurmukhi on every button.
2. Very low battery use. Tell me in plain words how much.
3. Notices from known groups, like the langar committee, that look different from messages by unknown people.
4. Notices that disappear on their own after their time.
5. Some way for someone who knows to set it up for me once, so I just open it.
6. A clear message on screen showing whether a post stayed in the camp or reached the outside.

**Deal-breakers:**
1. English-only, or typing-only.
2. Draining the battery or filling the storage.
3. The wipe button deleting the whole shared phone, or being easy to press by mistake.
4. Anyone, a stranger or the police, being able to see that I personally wrote something.
5. Feeling like a trick, meaning a file from unknown people or a link I can't check.

*Bas* (that's all). Make it work for a 52-year-old with dough on her hands, and the young ones will manage on their own.

## Follow-up interview

### Follow-up 1. You said that during the day the phone is with you and at night your son has it. Think back to the camp. What happened to the langar's messages and notices during the hours the phone was with him or back in the village? Who else, if anyone, did the committee rely on then?

At the camp the phone mostly stayed with me, because my son was in the village doing the *kheti*. But when I went home on my turn, the phone came with me. Then for the langar I was just a missed call. Messages sent to "Gurpreet Kaur" came to our house, and my son would say, "Bebe, someone is asking about atta again."

So the committee did not really depend on one phone. They depended on a few women who stayed long at the camp. One older *bibi* from the next village had her own phone and a very loud voice. Some of the boys in the sound-system tent kept a notebook. Each night the next day's numbers were written on the board outside the langar tent, so that whoever came in the morning could read them even if no phone had rung.

The real trouble was the messages that came at night, like "trolley will be late". Those got lost. They sat on a phone that was asleep or 300 km away. A notice that stays in the camp, and not on one person's phone, would have helped there.

### Follow-up 2. You told us your son can see everything on the phone, and that is normal for you. You also said a deal-breaker is anyone being able to see that you personally wrote something. Can you help me understand how those two sit together for you? Who do you mean by 'anyone', and who would be fine?

*Haan*, it sounds like two things, but for me it is one thing. My son is family. If he reads my voice notes, he is reading his mother. He already knows what I think. Same for my daughter, and the women I cooked with. They know my face, so there is nothing to hide from them.

By "anyone" I mean people outside that circle. A stranger in the camp who records everything. Someone who takes a screenshot, cuts it, and posts "look, this Gurpreet Kaur from such-and-such village said this". The people who made those ugly videos. And yes, officials, if they want to make a list of names. I did nothing wrong, but I don't want my name on anybody's list.

So for a notice like "need 10 people for roti", I don't need my name on it at all. Let it say "langar committee". If someone at the camp wants to know who wrote it, they can come and ask me to my face. That is how trust works with us. Your face is shown to people near you, and your name is not handed to everybody.

### Follow-up 3. When the voice note about 'police coming at 2 a.m.' arrived, what did you actually do in the next few minutes, and what did the women around you do? If that same note had shown up on a phone-to-phone notice board with a label saying it came from an unknown person, what do you think would have happened?

Honestly, the first minute, my heart jumped. We were lying under the blankets in the trolley. One girl started playing it loudly, and then everyone was awake. Two women began packing their bags. One started calling her husband. I said, "*Ruko* (wait). Who sent it?" It was forwarded from another group, and nobody knew where it first came from.

So I put on my shawl and walked to the stage tent, where the older men and the sound boys sleep. They said they had heard nothing, and the camp *pehredar* (night watchmen) said the road was quiet. I came back and told the women to sleep. Some slept. Some sat up till morning. Nothing happened.

If it had come marked "unknown person", I think half the women would still have been scared, because fear doesn't read labels. But it would have given me something to point at: "See, it says nobody knows who sent it." That helps the people who think. It would help even more if, ten minutes later, a notice from the committee could say "Checked. Road is quiet. Go to sleep."

### Follow-up 4. You said the langar committee's notices should look different from strangers' messages. In the camp, who decided who was 'the committee'? If a new person said they were from the langar, how did people check that, and has anyone ever pretended to be from a committee or a gurdwara?

Nobody voted, if that is what you mean. It came from the work. The village groups that brought rations sent one or two people each, and the women who cooked longest were automatically asked. The union people knew who was doing what. After a few weeks everybody knew the faces. "Committee" just meant the people who stood at the store tent with the register.

If a new person came saying "I am from langar", we checked the old way. Which village? Who sent you? Then someone would call that village, or ask a woman from near there. If nobody knew them, they could still do *sewa* washing pots, but they did not give orders or take ration money.

Has anyone pretended? Yes. Once, people came collecting donations "for the langar" on the road outside, and we never saw that money. Online I saw messages using a gurdwara's name to share things the gurdwara never said. So for your app, "committee" should not mean anyone who types the word. Someone we already trust should mark them, in front of us.

### Follow-up 5. You worried about pressing the wipe button by mistake and losing your son's photos. On a phone you share, what, if anything, would you ever want to remove quickly? And who should be allowed to decide that, you, your son, or both of you?

Not the photos, never. My grandchildren's pictures, my son's buffalo papers, the UPI app, those stay. If anything goes, it should only be this app's things: the notices, the camp messages, maybe the list of which people I talked to. That is protest business. The family business is separate.

If the button clears only the BuzzChat part, then I can decide that myself, because it is my sewa. If it touches anything of my son's, then no, we decide together, and it should not be possible for one tired thumb to do it alone.

Also, tell me in plain Punjabi what it will remove, before it removes it. "This will clear camp messages only. Your photos and WhatsApp are safe." And make it hard to press by accident. My hands are often wet or full of flour, and the phone sits in my dupatta, where buttons get pressed by themselves. My son once called someone from my pocket for ten minutes. I don't want my pocket deleting things.

### Follow-up 6. Imagine the young volunteers from the sound system installed this on your phone at the camp. What would you need to see or hear in the first week to keep it, and what would make you quietly stop opening it? Please tell me about a time you stopped using an app, or a group, that someone set up for you.

In the first week I need to hear something useful on it every day. The rota, the milk timing, "*dal* is finished, bring from store tent". If it sits empty, why would I open it? I need to send one voice note and see that the other women got it. And the battery should still be there in the evening.

What makes me stop: if it is silent, if it is full of boys posting jokes and speeches, if it keeps asking me questions in English, or if my son says "Bebe, your phone is hot, what is this?"

It has happened before. My granddaughter put a farming app on my phone with weather and *mandi* (market) prices. Good idea. But it was mostly English, full of advertisements, and it kept sending notifications at night. After a few days I just stopped touching it, and later my son deleted it for space. Same with a big union group with 900 members. It was so many forwards that I muted it, and then I never opened it again. I didn't complain to anyone. I just stopped. Most people do that.

### Follow-up 7. You said the line 'anyone scanning Bluetooth can tell the app is running' scared you. What did you picture when you heard that? Thinking about the camp, would just having the app be more worrying to you than having WhatsApp groups and protest videos on the phone?

I pictured a man walking slowly through the camp at night with a phone in his hand, and a little list on his screen: this tent, this tent, this trolley. Like a dog sniffing. I don't know if that is how it works. That is just what came into my head.

WhatsApp is on every phone in India, even the police officer's. Protest videos, everybody has them. My son has cricket videos and protest videos mixed together. So having those doesn't make me stand out. But an app that only protest people use, and that can be noticed from outside without touching my phone, feels different. It feels like wearing a badge in the dark.

But I am also being honest: if the whole camp used it, then it is like everyone wearing the same badge, and it means less. Fear comes from being one of only a few. So please tell people this plainly, without scaring and without hiding it, so that each person can decide for themselves.

### Follow-up 8. You said it should be made clear that this works inside the camp and won't reach home. If it only ever worked inside the camp, would it still be worth the battery and the storage to you? What would the langar have needed from it during those two days in January to say it was worth it?

Inside the camp only? Yes, still worth it, if it is light. The camp was not small. It went on for kilometres along the road, and walking from our langar to the store tent took a long time. Those two days, our biggest loss happened inside the camp, not between the camp and home.

What we needed: first, numbers. How many people were coming to eat, even roughly. Second, one place for the rota, so the shift that never came would have known the time had changed. Third, a way for the committee to say "this is checked" or "this is false" about rumours, with our label on it. Fourth, if one boy on a motorcycle reaches a place with signal, the message to the villages should go out with him, and replies should come back with him. That would be enough. I don't need it to reach Punjab by magic.

If it had saved one day of wasted food and one missing shift, I would say *theek hai* (alright), keep it. But not if it eats half my battery. Then the phone dies and we are back to paper anyway.
